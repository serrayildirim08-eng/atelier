## 0. Prefatory note — what this Part is, and is not

This Part is the doctrinal map of the E-2 treaty-investor classification: statute, regulations, FAM, USCIS Policy Manual, treaty-list status, and deference posture. It does not duplicate the practitioner-facing intake, packet-assembly, or RFE-response craft housed in `manuals/E2-PREPARATION-MANUAL.md`; rather, it is the doctrinal floor on which that craft operates. Where the practitioner manual asks "what do we file?", this Part asks Suárez-Lopez's first question: *what does the statute actually require, and what is the delegated authority for the agency's current practice?* The four sub-types (1 individual investor; 2 corporate-owned investor; 3 executive/supervisory employee; 4 essential-skills employee) are folded into the regulatory map per Supervisor scope (sub-type calibrations live in §§ 9.1–9.4 below, anchored to their FAM and 8 CFR provisions). Per Supervisor instruction, exemplars are anonymized to "Subtype N exemplar (descriptor, quarter)."

## 1. Treaty foundation — INA § 101(a)(15)(E)(ii)

### 1.1 Statutory text

The E-2 classification is a creature of treaty, not a creature of immigration policy in the ordinary sense. The Immigration and Nationality Act, codified at 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15)(E)(ii), defines the nonimmigrant treaty investor as an alien:

> "entitled to enter the United States under and in pursuance of the provisions of a treaty of commerce and navigation between the United States and the foreign state of which he is a national, and the spouse and children of any such alien if accompanying or following to join him … solely to develop and direct the operations of an enterprise in which he has invested, or of an enterprise in which he is actively in the process of investing, a substantial amount of capital."

(INA § 101(a)(15)(E)(ii); 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15)(E)(ii).)

Three textual features warrant emphasis. First, eligibility is conditioned on "a treaty of commerce and navigation" — an antecedent international instrument. Absent the treaty, there is no classification; the agency cannot create one by regulation. Second, the verb pair "develop and direct" is statutory, not regulatory. The agency's "develop and direct" gloss in 9 FAM 402.9-7 cannot lawfully expand or contract that statutory standard; it can only construe it. Third, "substantial amount of capital" is a statutory term of art whose contours Congress chose not to fix. That delegation is the doctrinal seam through which the proportionality (inverted-sliding-scale) construction enters. (See § 7.2 below.)

### 1.2 1952 codification

The classification was codified in its present form in the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952, Pub. L. No. 82-414, § 101(a)(15)(E), 66 Stat. 163, 168 (June 27, 1952). Before 1952, treaty-investor status existed only as a function of the underlying bilateral treaties of friendship, commerce, and navigation ("FCN treaties"); the 1952 Act regularized the visa category and created the statutory hook on which 8 CFR § 214.2(e) and 22 CFR § 41.51 were later built. The 1952 statute's structure — treaty antecedent, "develop and direct" duty, "substantial amount of capital" — has not materially changed.

### 1.3 IIRIRA and post-IIRIRA amendments

The Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996, Pub. L. No. 104-208, div. C, 110 Stat. 3009-546, made structural changes to admissibility, removal, and worksite enforcement, but it left the substantive E-2 elements untouched. Post-IIRIRA amendments germane to E-2 are limited and surgical:

- The American Competitiveness in the Twenty-First Century Act of 2000 (AC21), Pub. L. No. 106-313, did not directly amend INA § 101(a)(15)(E)(ii) but altered the procedural backdrop for nonimmigrant petitions filed under 8 CFR § 214.2.
- The E-3 (Australian specialty occupation) classification, added in 2005 by the REAL ID Act, Pub. L. No. 109-13, div. B, sits in § 101(a)(15)(E)(iii) and does not displace the (E)(ii) text.
- The James M. Inhofe National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2023, Pub. L. No. 117-263, § 5502, modified eligibility for treaty country status by requiring a specified minimum residency period in the treaty country — a 2022 statutory tightening with material consequences (see § 8.1).

The takeaway is doctrinal: when Congress wants to alter E-2, it amends the statute. The agencies' interpretive reach is correspondingly narrow.

## 2. Implementing regulations — 8 CFR § 214.2(e)

The principal implementing regulation is 8 CFR § 214.2(e). It is structured as a long single section with numbered sub-paragraphs, each addressing a discrete element. A section-by-section read follows, with the operative verb forms ("shall" vs "may") flagged.

### 2.1 § 214.2(e)(1) — General

Subparagraph (1) establishes the threshold: an alien who would otherwise be classifiable as a treaty trader or treaty investor "may be classified as such" upon meeting the substantive criteria. The operative verb is *may* — the regulation grants permissive eligibility, not a right. Adjudicatory discretion is preserved on the back end.

### 2.2 § 214.2(e)(2) — Treaty investor defined (Subtype 1 anchor)

Subparagraph (2) defines the treaty investor as "an alien who, in pursuance of the provisions of an applicable treaty of commerce and navigation … is seeking to enter the United States solely to develop and direct the operations of an enterprise in which he or she has invested, or is actively in the process of investing, a substantial amount of capital." This is the regulatory analogue of the statute. It is the anchor for **Subtype 1 (individual treaty investor)**: a single human, treaty-country national, ≥ 50 % owner of the Petitioner, who develops and directs.

### 2.3 § 214.2(e)(3) — Employee of treaty investor; corporate ownership (Subtype 2 anchor)

Subparagraph (3) addresses two related concepts: (i) the employee of a treaty investor, and (ii) the rule that where the treaty investor is not an individual but an enterprise, the enterprise must be at least 50 % owned by treaty-country nationals who, if not in the United States, would be eligible for E-2 status, or who, if in the United States, hold E-2 status. This is the **Subtype 2 (corporate-owned investor)** anchor: a treaty-country corporation invests in a US subsidiary, and the human Beneficiary qualifies through the corporate ownership chain. The operative verb is *shall* in the ownership-percentage rule — a hard floor, not a discretionary minimum.

### 2.4 § 214.2(e)(12) — At-risk; impermissible loans

Subparagraph (12) codifies the at-risk doctrine and excludes loans secured by the assets of the enterprise from the substantiality calculation. The text reads: investment capital "must be subject to partial or total loss if investment fortunes reverse." The doctrinal lineage runs to *Matter of Walsh & Pollard*, 20 I&N Dec. 60 (BIA 1988), discussed by my colleague Prof. Yılmaz in Part 2.

### 2.5 § 214.2(e)(14) — Substantiality

Subparagraph (14) sets the proportionality test (the "inverted sliding scale"). Substantiality is measured against (i) the total cost of an established enterprise or the total amount required to establish a new one, (ii) the amount normally considered substantial in the relevant industry, and (iii) the amount sufficient to ensure the investor's commitment. There is no fixed dollar threshold — a deliberate regulatory choice that Congress has not displaced.

### 2.6 § 214.2(e)(15) — Marginality

Subparagraph (15) codifies marginality: the enterprise "must not be marginal." A marginal enterprise is one that lacks the present or future capacity to generate more than enough income to provide a minimal living for the treaty investor and family. The five-year horizon — borrowed by analogy from *Matter of Ho*, 19 I&N Dec. 582 (BIA 1988) — is regulatory practice rather than statutory text.

### 2.7 § 214.2(e)(17) — Executive or supervisory employee (Subtype 3 anchor)

Subparagraph (17) governs the executive or supervisory employee of a qualifying treaty enterprise. The position must be one of "ultimate control and responsibility for the enterprise's overall operation, or a major component thereof." This is the **Subtype 3** regulatory anchor. The verb is mandatory ("must"); the proof, factual.

### 2.8 § 214.2(e)(18) — Essential skills employee (Subtype 4 anchor)

Subparagraph (18) governs the essential-skills (or "specialized knowledge") employee. The text:

> "An alien employee with special qualifications may be classified as a treaty investor employee if the alien's services are essential to the efficient operation of the enterprise."

This is the **Subtype 4** anchor. Note: *may be classified* — permissive on its face, but the FAM gloss at 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(b) constructs a multi-factor test that imports something close to mandatoriness in adjudicative practice. (See § 7.3 on the deference dialectic.)

### 2.9 § 214.2(e)(19)–(23) — Period of stay; change of employer; reverse extensions

Subparagraphs (19) through (23) handle the procedural mechanics of period of stay, change of employer (subject to E-classification rules), and reverse extension. The two-year admission period for E-2 is regulatory, not statutory; it can be (and historically has been) altered by reciprocity schedule changes — a point that becomes operative when Congress or the Department of State adjusts a country's reciprocity table.

## 3. State Department guidance — 9 FAM 402.9

### 3.1 Hierarchy and structure

The Foreign Affairs Manual at 9 FAM 402.9 is the primary consular-side construction of E-classification. Its sub-section hierarchy is doctrinally organized:

| FAM section | Topic |
|---|---|
| 9 FAM 402.9-3 | Overview and general principles |
| 9 FAM 402.9-4 | Nationality (applicant + enterprise) |
| 9 FAM 402.9-5 | Investment defined |
| 9 FAM 402.9-6 | Substantial / real and operating / marginality |
| 9 FAM 402.9-7 | Develop and direct (investor); employee variants |
| 9 FAM 402.9-8 | Dependents |
| 9 FAM 402.9-9 | Extensions and renewals |

### 3.2 9 FAM 402.9-4 — Nationality

Sub-section -4(A) addresses the applicant's nationality: the applicant must possess the nationality of the treaty country. Dual nationality is permissible only if the *treaty* nationality is operative on the application. Sub-section -4(B) addresses the enterprise's nationality: at least 50 % of the enterprise must be owned by treaty-country nationals who, if outside the US, would be eligible for E-2, or who, if inside, hold E-2 status. Sub-section -4(F) addresses nonimmigrant intent — the applicant's bona fide intent to depart upon termination of E-2 status.

The 50 % rule is binary: at 49.999 %, the enterprise fails. It is not a sliding scale.

### 3.3 9 FAM 402.9-6 — Substantial, real and operating, more than marginal

Sub-section -6 is the heart of the FAM construction:

- **-6(A) Bona fide enterprise.** "A real and active commercial or entrepreneurial undertaking, producing some service or commodity. It cannot be a paper organization or an idle speculative investment held for potential appreciation."
- **-6(B) At-risk / irrevocable commitment.** Funds must be "irrevocably committed" and "subject to partial or total loss if investment fortunes reverse."
- **-6(C) Substantiality (proportionality).** The inverted sliding scale: smaller enterprises require a higher percentage of capital; larger enterprises a lower percentage. There is no fixed dollar floor.
- **-6(D) Source of funds.** The funds must be the applicant's own and lawfully obtained. The chain (origin → intermediate accounts → US deposit → deployment) must be documentable.
- **-6(E) Marginality.** The enterprise must have the present or future capacity (typically within five years) to generate more than minimal living for the applicant and family. The five-year horizon is FAM convention; adjudicators apply it as if controlling.

The verbatim language at -6(E) bears quotation:

> "A marginal enterprise is one that does not have the present or future capacity to generate more than enough income to provide a minimal living for the treaty investor and his or her family. An enterprise that does not have the capacity to generate such income, but that has a present or future capacity to make a significant economic contribution, is not a marginal enterprise."

The "significant economic contribution" escape hatch is doctrinally important and frequently under-cited in petitions.

### 3.4 9 FAM 402.9-7 — Develop and direct; employee variants

Sub-section -7(1) addresses the treaty investor's "develop and direct" duty: the applicant must possess "controlling interest" — typically ≥ 50 % ownership, or operational control via a managerial position. This is the **Subtype 1** anchor on the FAM side.

Sub-section -7(2) addresses employees, with two variants:

- **-7(2)(a) Executive or supervisory.** Position must entail "great authority to determine the policy and direction" of the enterprise; routine staff work with incidental supervision does not qualify. **Subtype 3** anchor.
- **-7(2)(b) Essential skills.** Multi-factor test: degree of expertise; whether others possess the same skill; length of training/experience; relation to enterprise's specific processes; salary the skill commands; availability in the US labor market; foreign-language-and-culture-alone is insufficient. **Subtype 4** anchor.

The FAM at -7(2)(b) flags a temporal dimension: skills essential at start-up may not remain essential after operations stabilize. The drafter who ignores this temporal point invites a Notice of Intent to Deny (NOID).

## 4. Consular regulations — 22 CFR § 41.51

The Department of State's regulations at 22 CFR § 41.51 govern issuance of the E-1 / E-2 visa at consulates abroad. Three points warrant emphasis.

### 4.1 22 CFR § 41.51(b)(1)–(11) — Substantive requirements

The regulation enumerates the substantive E-2 requirements in language closely paralleling the INA and 8 CFR. § 41.51(b)(8) (substantiality) defines the "substantial amount of capital" standard with explicit reference to the inverted sliding scale: capital is substantial when "sufficient to ensure the treaty investor's financial commitment to the successful operation of the enterprise."

### 4.2 22 CFR § 41.51(c) — Derivative dependents

The spouse and unmarried children under 21 of an E-2 principal are derivatively classifiable under (c). The 2022 USCIS policy guidance (PA-2022-04) extended automatic employment authorization to E-2 spouses incident to status — codified administratively at 8 CFR § 274a.12(a)(17) — eliminating the prior I-765 requirement. The dependent classification itself is *mandatory* upon proof of qualifying relationship and accompanying-or-following-to-join status; it is not discretionary.

### 4.3 MRV fee

The Machine Readable Visa (MRV) fee for an E-2 application is set by Department of State fee schedule under 22 CFR § 22.1, currently USD 315.00 per applicant (subject to reciprocal-fee adjustment for certain treaty countries). The fee is non-refundable regardless of adjudicative outcome. Reciprocity-fee adjustments are published in the country reciprocity tables and override the base MRV fee where applicable.

## 5. USCIS Policy Manual — Vol. 2, Pt. F

The USCIS Policy Manual at Volume 2, Part F addresses "Employment-Based Nonimmigrants — Treaty Traders and Investors." It is doctrinally junior to 8 CFR § 214.2(e) and 9 FAM 402.9 (which control consular adjudication) but governs USCIS-side adjudication of I-129 petitions filed for change-of-status (COS) and extension postures.

The Policy Manual cross-references run as follows:

- Vol. 2, Pt. F, Ch. 1 — Purpose and statutory authority (anchors back to INA § 101(a)(15)(E) and 8 CFR § 214.2(e)).
- Vol. 2, Pt. F, Ch. 2 — Treaty Trader (E-1) — out of scope for this Part (separate module).
- Vol. 2, Pt. F, Ch. 3 — Treaty Investor (E-2) — substantive elements.
- Vol. 2, Pt. F, Ch. 4 — Period of stay, extensions, change of employer.
- Vol. 2, Pt. F, Ch. 5 — Family members.

The Policy Manual incorporates 9 FAM 402.9 by reference for substantive criteria, with one important caveat: USCIS does not consider itself bound by FAM constructions that the Department of State alone authored. The result is a doctrinal asymmetry — consular officers apply the FAM as binding; USCIS officers treat it as persuasive. This asymmetry is the operative reason a Subtype 1 case may be approved at a US consulate but receive an RFE on the same record at a USCIS service center, and vice versa.

## 7. Discretion mapping — mandatory vs discretionary; deference posture

### 7.1 Mandatory vs discretionary

A close read of the operative verbs across the stack:

| Provision | Verb | Character |
|---|---|---|
| INA § 101(a)(15)(E)(ii) — eligibility | "shall be" (entitled) | Mandatory eligibility upon satisfaction |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(1) — classification | "may be classified" | Permissive |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(2) — investor definition | "is" / "shall" | Definitional / mandatory |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(12) — at-risk | "must" | Mandatory |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(14) — substantiality | "must be" | Mandatory |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(15) — marginality | "must not be" | Mandatory negative |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(17) — exec/supervisory | "must be" | Mandatory |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(18) — essential skills | "may be classified" | Permissive |
| 9 FAM 402.9-6(C) — substantiality | "is to be" | Construction-of-mandatory |
| 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(b) — essential skills | "should consider" | Persuasive multi-factor |

The doctrinal bottom line: the *eligibility* of a qualifying applicant is mandatory; the *classification* of the applicant into a sub-category is permissive on its face but constrained by the multi-factor tests. Adjudicative discretion lives most powerfully in (a) substantiality (no fixed floor), (b) marginality (future-capacity construction), and (c) essential skills (multi-factor proof).

### 7.2 Chevron, Skidmore, and Loper Bright — the post-2024 deference posture

The Supreme Court's decision in *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo*, 144 S. Ct. 2244 (2024), overruled *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. NRDC*, 467 U.S. 837 (1984), and reinstated the courts' independent obligation to interpret statutes. The doctrinal consequence for E-2 is significant though not revolutionary:

- The *statutory* terms of art ("substantial amount of capital," "develop and direct," "treaty of commerce and navigation") are now subject to *de novo* judicial construction without obligatory deference to agency interpretations.
- Long-standing agency *interpretive* glosses — particularly the FAM's inverted-sliding-scale construction of substantiality and the five-year marginality horizon — retain *Skidmore* persuasive weight (per *Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134 (1944)) to the extent of their "power to persuade" — i.e., their consistency, formality, thoroughness of consideration, and contemporaneity with the statute.
- Where the statute is silent and the agency has interpreted by formal regulation (8 CFR), the regulation remains controlling unless successfully challenged under the APA.

Practical consequence for our practice: pre-2024 boilerplate that cited Chevron should be retired. RFE responses and APA challenges should now anchor in (i) the statutory text directly, (ii) the regulation as authoritative absent APA vulnerability, and (iii) the FAM as Skidmore-persuasive. Loper Bright did not invalidate any specific E-2 regulation; it altered the litigation posture for challenging interpretive overreach. The regulatory architecture remains intact; the deference floor moved.

### 7.3 Where Loper Bright bites hardest in E-2

Three doctrinal pressure points:

1. **Substantiality.** The FAM's inverted sliding scale is a construction, not statutory text. A future federal-court challenge could argue that "substantial" should be construed against ordinary meaning, not via the agency's proportionality rubric. The agency's construction is venerable and consistently applied — Skidmore weight is high — but no longer Chevron-immune.
2. **Marginality "future capacity."** The five-year horizon is regulatory practice, not statutory text. A litigant might argue any reasonable horizon (10 years; the enterprise's natural maturation cycle) suffices.
3. **Essential skills "multi-factor."** The FAM's multi-factor test at 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(b) is persuasive only post-Loper Bright. Adjudicators applying it as if controlling are vulnerable to APA challenge for arbitrary-and-capricious application of a non-binding gloss.

None of these pressure points has produced a successful federal challenge as of 2026-04-29 — *flag for Supervisor: monitor for first post-Loper Bright E-2 federal decision, expected in trailing 12–18 months.*

## 9. Sub-type regulatory anchors (folded in per Supervisor scope)

The four E-2 sub-types are doctrinally distinct configurations of the same statutory and regulatory stack. Each anchors to specific FAM and 8 CFR provisions.

### 9.1 Subtype 1 — Individual treaty investor

**Anchor.** 9 FAM 402.9-7(1) + 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(2).

**Doctrinal frame.** A single human Beneficiary, treaty-country national, owns ≥ 50 % of the Petitioner directly or holds operational control via managerial governance, and personally develops and directs the enterprise. The investment chain is personal: lawful funds (sale proceeds, savings, gift, inheritance, non-collateralized loan) traced from origin to US deployment. **Subtype 1 exemplar (US LLC, Q1 2026 filing)** illustrates the modal pattern: small US LLC, six-figure investment, sole or co-owner Beneficiary, individual SOF chain.

**Mandatory provisions.** 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(2) (definition); (e)(12) (at-risk); (e)(14) (substantiality); (e)(15) (marginality). 9 FAM 402.9-4(A) (nationality), 402.9-4(B) (≥ 50 %), 402.9-6(A)–(E), 402.9-7(1).

**Discretion locus.** Substantiality (no fixed floor) and marginality (future-capacity construction) are the two adjudicator-facing soft elements. The cover-letter argument must close both via documented industry comparison and a five-year financial projection that crosses minimal-living plus a margin.

### 9.2 Subtype 2 — Corporate-owned investor

**Anchor.** 9 FAM 402.9-4(B) + 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(3).

**Doctrinal frame.** A treaty-country corporation invests in a US subsidiary; the human Beneficiary qualifies through the corporate ownership chain. Two ownership layers must clear the ≥ 50 % treaty test: the foreign parent's ownership by treaty-country nationals, and the US subsidiary's ownership by the foreign parent. Both layers must trace.

**Mandatory provisions.** 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(3) (corporate ownership); 9 FAM 402.9-4(B) (50 % rule applied to enterprise nationality). Investment substantiality and marginality apply at the US-subsidiary level.

**Discretion locus.** The ownership trace is technically demanding but not discretionary; it is satisfied or not on the documents (share registers, board resolutions, capital tables). Discretion concentrates at substantiality (parent-to-subsidiary capitalization) and at the US subsidiary's bona fide-enterprise proof.

### 9.3 Subtype 3 — Executive / supervisory employee

**Anchor.** 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(a) + 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(17).

**Doctrinal frame.** The Petitioner enterprise must already qualify (under Subtype 1 or Subtype 2 logic). The Beneficiary is an executive (C-suite, VP, Director, Country Manager) with "great authority to determine the policy and direction" of the enterprise (9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(a)). The role test is qualitative, not titular; an officer in title who performs routine staff work fails. The regulatory trap is § 214.2(e)(17)'s "ultimate control and responsibility" language — adjudicators read this as requiring substantive authority, not nominal.

**Mandatory provisions.** 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(17). 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(a) (multi-factor: title, organizational placement, span of control, supervised employees' skill, salary, prior experience).

**Discretion locus.** All seven multi-factor elements at 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(a) are assessed on the record. The cover-letter draft must address each factor head-on; absence of a factor (e.g., span-of-control evidence) is a documented hole, not a permissible omission. The "anti-routine paragraph" — explicitly disclaiming substantive staff work as the Beneficiary's primary duty — is a doctrinal best practice anchored in the FAM's express exclusion of routine work.

### 9.4 Subtype 4 — Essential-skills employee

**Anchor.** 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(b) + 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(18).

**Doctrinal frame.** Same enterprise-qualifying foundation as Subtype 3. The Beneficiary's specialized knowledge or skill must be essential to the efficient operation of the enterprise. The proof is the FAM's nine-factor test: degree of expertise; uniqueness; length of training/experience; training period required; relation to enterprise's specific processes; salary the skill commands; availability in US labor market; foreign-language-and-culture-alone-insufficient; durability of the essentiality. **Subtype 4 exemplar (US subsidiary, energy sector, Q1 2024 filing)** illustrates the modal pattern: foreign parent in a specialized industrial sector, US subsidiary, Beneficiary holds technical product/market knowledge not readily available in US labor market, salary differential vs US peer market documented.

**Mandatory provisions.** 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(18). 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(b) (multi-factor).

**Discretion locus.** Two pressure points. First, "not readily available in US labor market" (§ 214.2(e)(18)) — adjudicators expect labor-market evidence (BLS data, industry surveys, recruiting records). Second, the *temporal* dimension at FAM -7(2)(b): start-up skills may not remain essential after operations stabilize. The drafter must either argue durable essentiality (product improvement, quality control, unique-service activities) or honestly request limited-duration status. Misalignment between the temporal frame and the requested period of stay is a NOID predictor.

### 9.5 Sub-type interaction matrix

A single matter may present sub-type ambiguity. The doctrinal calibrators:

| Signal | Subtype |
|---|---|
| Single human ≥ 50 % owner of US LLC, personal SOF chain | 1 |
| Treaty-country corporate parent → US subsidiary; human is the controlling shareholder of the parent | 2 |
| Treaty-country parent → US subsidiary; human is an officer of the US subsidiary, not a controlling shareholder | 3 or 4 |
| Officer holds P&L authority and supervises substantial staff | 3 |
| Officer holds technical / product / market specialization, salary differential vs US peer market | 4 |
| Misclassification risk: BUS folder label or "investor" intake but actual fact pattern is employee-of-treaty-enterprise | Re-classify to 3 or 4 |

Misclassification defeats cases. The regulatory anchors above govern; intake labels do not.

## 10. Closing doctrinal observations

Three observations close this Part.

First, the E-2 regulatory architecture is statutorily thin and regulatorily thick. The INA hands the agencies a brief mandate ("substantial," "develop and direct"); the agencies have constructed a multi-decade gloss in 8 CFR § 214.2(e), 22 CFR § 41.51, and 9 FAM 402.9. Loper Bright did not destabilize that gloss but moved the deference floor. Practitioners should now cite to statute, regulation, and FAM in that hierarchy — and stop reflexively citing Chevron.

Second, the four sub-types are doctrinally distinct configurations, not procedural variants. Choosing the wrong sub-type produces a record that fails to clear the elements of the correct sub-type — even if the underlying facts are strong. The regulatory anchors (§ 9 above) are the calibration tool.

Third, the trailing 24 months show no major regulatory upheaval, but the post-2024 deference shift, the 2022 NDAA residency rule, and the 2022 spouse-EAD policy together remake the practice quietly. Each must be carried in every cover letter that touches its facts.
