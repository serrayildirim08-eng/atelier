## 1. Treaty foundation — INA § 101(a)(15)(E)(ii)

### 1.1 Statutory text — verbatim, with paragraph-by-paragraph parsing

The E-2 classification is — and this is the doctrinal first principle — a creature of treaty, not of immigration policy in the ordinary sense. It exists because the Congress, by an act of statutory grace exercised in 1952, lent immigration consequence to a pre-existing international instrument. The statute, codified at Section 101(a)(15)(E)(ii) of the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952 ("INA"), 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15)(E)(ii), defines as a nonimmigrant alien:

> "an alien entitled to enter the United States under and in pursuance of the provisions of a treaty of commerce and navigation between the United States and the foreign state of which he is a national, and the spouse and children of any such alien if accompanying or following to join him … solely to develop and direct the operations of an enterprise in which he has invested, or of an enterprise in which he is actively in the process of investing, a substantial amount of capital."

(INA § 101(a)(15)(E)(ii); 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15)(E)(ii).)

The statutory text rewards close textualist parsing. I will walk it phrase by phrase.

**"an alien."** The classification is conferred on a natural person. The definitional architecture of the INA at § 101(a)(3) defines "alien" as "any person not a citizen or national of the United States." The classification is consequently unavailable to a US person; it is, by definition, an entry classification for an outsider seeking lawful admission. The interaction with derivative citizenship (which can defeat alienage retroactively) is handled at § 101(a)(22) and is operationally rare in the E-2 context.

**"entitled to enter the United States."** The verb "entitled" is a term of art in nonimmigrant law. It is not "permitted" or "eligible"; it is "entitled." The choice imposes on the agency the obligation to admit the qualifying applicant, subject only to the consular officer's residual authority to find inadmissibility under § 212(a) and the limited grounds for revocation. This is mandatory eligibility upon proof — what I refer to in § 7.1 below as the "eligibility-mandatory / classification-permissive" bifurcation.

**"under and in pursuance of the provisions of a treaty of commerce and navigation."** This is the treaty hook — the doctrinal crux. The classification has no independent existence; it is parasitic upon a bilateral instrument. Three sub-points warrant attention:

1. The instrument must be a "treaty of commerce and navigation." The 1952 Congress used this phrase in its mid-twentieth-century usage, which embraced the classic Friendship, Commerce, and Navigation ("FCN") treaty form. Modern Bilateral Investment Treaties ("BITs") were not contemplated at codification; they were assimilated by Department of State practice and judicial gloss in the late twentieth century, on the theory that a BIT is functionally a "treaty of commerce and navigation" for E-2 purposes. The Supreme Court has not directly passed on the assimilation, but it has been uncontroverted in practice for over four decades. (See § 1.4 below, treaty hook doctrine.)
2. The instrument must be "between the United States and the foreign state of which he is a national." This is the bilateralism principle: a multilateral commercial agreement (e.g., the WTO agreements, the Trans-Pacific Partnership in its various forms) does not, of itself, qualify. Eligibility runs through bilateral consent.
3. "Foreign state" is a term of art that imports the recognition doctrine: only states recognized by the United States qualify. A breakaway region, an unrecognized polity, or a successor state lacking US recognition does not generate E-2 eligibility, even if the antecedent FCN treaty was signed with the predecessor.

**"between the United States and the foreign state of which he is a national."** Nationality is the chain that connects the alien to the treaty. The applicant must "be a national" of the treaty country. Nationality is determined by reference to the law of the foreign state — see *Matter of M—*, 1 I&N Dec. 19 (Att'y Gen. 1940) (nationality determined under foreign law) — but is constrained by the dual-nationality rule that the treaty nationality must be operatively claimed at adjudication. Section 1.4 below treats the *Charming Betsy* canon and treaty self-execution; § 9.2 treats the corporate ownership chain that imputes treaty nationality through entities.

**"and the spouse and children of any such alien if accompanying or following to join him."** This is the derivative-classification clause. The spouse and unmarried children under 21 derive E-2 status from the principal. The clause confers derivative classification *as a matter of statutory entitlement*, not regulatory discretion. The derivative is, in effect, statutorily mandatory. The 2022 work-authorization extension to E-2 spouses (see § 8.2 below) operationalizes the derivative clause; it does not create the entitlement, which existed since 1952.

**"solely to develop and direct the operations of an enterprise."** The adverb "solely" is doctrinally significant. It limits the principal's purpose of admission to the develop-and-direct duty; collateral activities (other employment, study not incident to the develop-and-direct role) are by statute outside the classification. The agency has historically been generous in construing what activities are "solely" within develop-and-direct (e.g., social, civic, and incidental activities are tolerated), but a substantive second occupation will defeat the classification.

The verb pair "develop and direct" is statutory, not regulatory. Its construction is the subject of § 1.5 below.

**"in which he has invested, or of an enterprise in which he is actively in the process of investing."** Two temporal postures are admitted: (i) investment completed; (ii) investment in active progress. The "actively in the process of investing" prong is the doctrinal home of the irrevocable-commitment principle: the applicant must have committed funds beyond mere speculation but need not have completed every disbursement. The regulation at 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(12) operationalizes this temporal bifurcation. (See § 2.13 below.)

**"a substantial amount of capital."** The crowning ambiguity. Congress chose not to fix a numerical threshold; the statute commits the construction to the agencies. This is the doctrinal seam through which the inverted-sliding-scale construction (see § 1.6 below and § 2.15 below) lawfully enters.

Three textual features warrant emphasis as a matter of statutory architecture. First, eligibility is conditioned on "a treaty of commerce and navigation" — an antecedent international instrument. Absent the treaty, there is no classification; the agency cannot create one by regulation. Second, the verb pair "develop and direct" is statutory, not regulatory. The agency's "develop and direct" gloss in 9 FAM 402.9-7 cannot lawfully expand or contract that statutory standard; it can only construe it. Third, "substantial amount of capital" is a statutory term of art whose contours Congress chose not to fix. That delegation is the doctrinal seam through which the proportionality (inverted-sliding-scale) construction lawfully enters. (See § 7.2 below for the post-*Loper Bright* analysis of the construction.)

### 1.1.1 Comparison to (E)(i) and (E)(iii)

The (E)(ii) classification sits within a three-clause family. To understand what (E)(ii) *requires*, one must see what its sister clauses *do not* require.

**INA § 101(a)(15)(E)(i) — treaty trader.** The treaty trader classification, codified contemporaneously with the treaty investor, defines an alien:

> "entitled to enter the United States under and in pursuance of the provisions of a treaty of commerce and navigation between the United States and the foreign state of which he is a national … solely to carry on substantial trade, including trade in services or trade in technology, principally between the United States and the foreign state of which he is a national …"

The (E)(i) clause shares the treaty hook, the nationality requirement, the "solely" purpose limitation, and the bilateralism principle. It diverges in the substantive operating standard: (E)(i) requires "substantial trade … principally between the United States and the foreign state," whereas (E)(ii) requires "develop and direct" of an enterprise in which the alien has invested "a substantial amount of capital." The (E)(i) classification is a trade-flow classification; the (E)(ii) classification is an enterprise-equity classification. Both are gated by the same treaty inventory (subject to specific treaty-by-treaty reservations limiting one or the other).

**INA § 101(a)(15)(E)(iii) — Australian specialty occupation.** Added by § 501 of the REAL ID Act of 2005, Pub. L. No. 109-13, div. B, 119 Stat. 231, 322 (May 11, 2005), the (E)(iii) classification defines an alien:

> "entitled to enter the United States solely to perform services in a specialty occupation in the United States if the alien is a national of the Commonwealth of Australia and with respect to whom the Secretary of Labor determines and certifies to the Secretary of Homeland Security and the Secretary of State that the intending employer has filed with the Secretary of Labor an attestation under section 212(t)(1)."

The (E)(iii) classification is materially different in three respects: (i) it is country-specific rather than treaty-pooled; (ii) it imports the H-1B "specialty occupation" framework rather than the treaty-trader / treaty-investor framework; (iii) it requires Department of Labor attestation. The (E)(iii) clause was a political compromise in the bilateral negotiation of the US–Australia Free Trade Agreement; it is doctrinally a classification of its own, sharing only its E-classification numbering with (E)(i) and (E)(ii). For our purposes, (E)(iii) is out of scope; it is mentioned here only to clarify that the E-2 classification rules do not extend to it.

The structural lesson: the (E) family is a statutory grouping of three classifications with a shared treaty-of-commerce-and-navigation foundation but materially different substantive elements. Misapplication is doctrinally easy if a practitioner reads "E classification" as undifferentiated; it is not. (E)(ii) is, and remains, the singular treaty-investor clause.

### 1.2 Legislative history — from 1875 to 1952

The E-2 classification did not spring fully formed from the McCarran-Walter Act. Its statutory antecedents trace to nineteenth-century commercial diplomacy.

**The 1875 Treaty of Friendship, Commerce, and Navigation backdrop.** The United States entered into a series of FCN treaties beginning in the early Republic. The 1815 commerce convention with Great Britain is the earliest still-operative instrument relevant to E-2 eligibility (the United Kingdom remains an E-2 country under that 1815 convention as supplemented and never displaced). The mid-nineteenth-century FCN treaties (with the Ottoman Empire, with various Latin American republics, with European powers) created reciprocal commercial rights for nationals of the contracting states. Crucially, however, the FCN treaties of the nineteenth century did not, by their terms, create immigration entitlements. They created commercial entitlements — the right to trade, to own property, to engage in business — within the framework of each contracting state's general immigration laws. Immigration *qua* immigration was not the subject; it was the predicate.

This pre-codification posture matters because it explains the McCarran-Walter Act's choice in 1952 to create a *bridge* statute. Congress in 1952 did not create the underlying right to enter for trade and investment — that right (or its sketch) existed in the FCN treaties. Congress created the *immigration-law mechanism* through which the antecedent treaty rights would be vindicated. The 1952 codification is, doctrinally, an enabling provision: it tells the consular and immigration agencies what to do when an alien presents under an FCN treaty (or successor instrument) that grants commercial rights.

**The Immigration Act of 1924 and the precursor regime.** Before 1952, the closest statutory analogue to the modern E classification was the "treaty merchant" provision under § 3(6) of the Immigration Act of 1924 (the Johnson-Reed Act), Pub. L. No. 68-139, 43 Stat. 153. The 1924 Act created a non-quota classification for aliens "entitled to enter the United States solely to carry on trade under and in pursuance of the provisions of a present existing treaty of commerce and navigation." That language is plainly the textual ancestor of the modern (E)(i) clause — the "treaty trader" classification. There was no corresponding statutory provision for the modern (E)(ii) treaty-investor classification under the 1924 Act; treaty-investor admissions were processed under the broader "treaty merchant" framework or under generic visitor-for-business posture.

The doctrinal distinction between trade and investment was not statutorily crystallized until 1952. Before 1952, an investor-only applicant (no trade flow) could be denied as outside the "treaty merchant" rubric. The 1952 separation of (E)(i) and (E)(ii) was a deliberate accommodation of the post–World War II investment posture, in which European and Asian capital seeking US deployment did not always travel with reciprocal trade flows. The 1952 Congress saw the gap; it filled it.

**The McCarran-Walter Act of 1952 — committee history.** The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952, Pub. L. No. 82-414, 66 Stat. 163 (June 27, 1952), was the product of a multi-year drafting effort principally led by Senator Pat McCarran (D-Nev.) and Representative Francis Walter (D-Pa.). The committee history of the E classification is sparse — it was a non-controversial provision in a statute consumed by debates over national-origins quotas, internal security, and asylum. The Senate Judiciary Committee's report on the bill (S. Rep. No. 82-1137 (1952)) and the House Judiciary Committee's report (H.R. Rep. No. 82-1365 (1952), reprinted in 1952 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1653) treat the E classification briefly; both characterize it as a continuation and refinement of the pre-existing treaty merchant framework. Neither report engages closely with the "develop and direct" language or the "substantial amount of capital" delegation. Contemporary commentary in the immigration bar likewise characterizes the E provisions as "uncontroversial restatements of existing treaty practice." (See, e.g., contemporary annotations to the 1952 Act collected in Charles Gordon & Harry N. Rosenfield, *Immigration Law and Procedure* (1959 ed., later editions).)

The legislative history's silence is itself informative. The 1952 Congress did not engage closely with the substantive elements of the E-2 classification because it was content to delegate construction to the agencies under the antecedent treaty framework. The agency interpretive thicket that developed in the following decades — the FAM at 9 FAM 402.9, the regulation at 8 CFR § 214.2(e), the consular regulation at 22 CFR § 41.51 — was, in this sense, anticipated by the brevity of the 1952 statute. Congress wrote a frame; the agencies filled it. After *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo*, 144 S. Ct. 2244 (2024), the doctrinal weight of that filling is diminished but not eliminated. (See § 7.2 below.)

**The 1952 statute's structure — verbatim from 66 Stat. 163.** The statute at § 101(a)(15)(E) as enacted reads:

> "An alien entitled to enter the United States under and in pursuance of the provisions of a treaty of commerce and navigation between the United States and the foreign state of which he is a national, and the spouse and children of any such alien if accompanying or following to join him: (i) solely to carry on substantial trade, principally between the United States and the foreign state of which he is a national; or (ii) solely to develop and direct the operations of an enterprise in which he has invested, or of an enterprise in which he is actively in the process of investing, a substantial amount of capital …"

(§ 101(a)(15)(E), 66 Stat. 168.) The 1952 text, with the addition of (E)(iii) in 2005 and the trade-in-services / trade-in-technology amendment to (E)(i) in 1990, is the text that controls today.

### 1.3 Post-1952 developments — every amendment chronicled

Post-1952, the E-2 statutory text has been modified narrowly and surgically. The amendments that bear on E-2 doctrine are catalogued below in chronological order.

**Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (Mar. 17, 1980).** The Refugee Act restructured the refugee and asylum provisions of the INA but did not amend (E)(ii). Its relevance to E-2 doctrine is collateral: by clarifying the asylum framework at INA § 208, the Refugee Act removed a doctrinal ambiguity about whether E-2 status could be combined with a pending asylum application. The post-1980 posture is that an E-2 applicant with a pending asylum claim faces a doctrinal inconsistency between the bona-fide-nonimmigrant intent embedded in E-2 (see 9 FAM 402.9-4(F)) and the indefinite-stay implication of asylum. The agencies have generally resolved the inconsistency by treating the asylum filing as a presumptive defeat of nonimmigrant intent, though the doctrine is fact-bound.

**Immigration Act of 1990, Pub. L. No. 101-649, § 204, 104 Stat. 4978, 5018 (Nov. 29, 1990).** The 1990 Act amended (E)(i) to add the phrase "including trade in services or trade in technology" to the substantial-trade requirement. The amendment did not touch (E)(ii). Its E-2 relevance is consequently nil but bears mention because practitioners sometimes confuse the (E)(i) trade-in-services accommodation for an (E)(ii) substance-of-investment accommodation; they are unrelated.

**Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 (IIRIRA), Pub. L. No. 104-208, div. C, 110 Stat. 3009-546 (Sept. 30, 1996).** IIRIRA made structural changes to admissibility, removal, and worksite enforcement, but it left the substantive (E)(ii) elements untouched. The IIRIRA-era amendments to § 212 and § 240 affect E-2 collaterally — primarily through changes to the unlawful-presence framework (§ 212(a)(9)(B) and (C)) that affect E-2 applicants who have overstayed prior status. The substantive E-2 elements are unchanged.

**American Competitiveness in the Twenty-First Century Act of 2000 ("AC21"), Pub. L. No. 106-313, 114 Stat. 1251 (Oct. 17, 2000).** AC21 did not directly amend INA § 101(a)(15)(E)(ii). It altered the procedural backdrop for nonimmigrant petitions filed under 8 CFR § 214.2 generally, including premium-processing availability for I-129 petitions in the E classification (premium processing for I-129 E-2 petitions was added by USCIS regulation in subsequent years; AC21 itself authorized the premium-processing fee structure). The substantive E-2 elements are unchanged.

**REAL ID Act of 2005, Pub. L. No. 109-13, div. B, 119 Stat. 231, 322 (May 11, 2005).** The REAL ID Act added (E)(iii) (Australian specialty occupation, supra § 1.1.1). It did not amend (E)(ii). Its inclusion in this catalog is for completeness only.

**National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2023 ("NDAA 2023"), Pub. L. No. 117-263, § 5502, 136 Stat. 2395, 3253 (Dec. 23, 2022).** NDAA 2023 § 5502 — the James M. Inhofe NDAA — modified eligibility for treaty-country status by requiring a specified minimum residency period in the treaty country before E-2 eligibility attaches. The provision is operationally directed at "citizenship-by-investment" programs in certain countries (Grenada, Türkiye for naturalized-by-investment, Bulgaria, and others) and imposes a three-year residency requirement on naturalized treaty-country nationals before they can claim E-2 eligibility. The statutory text reads:

> "An alien shall not be considered a national of a foreign state for purposes of section 101(a)(15)(E) of the Immigration and Nationality Act … unless the alien has been domiciled in the foreign state for a continuous period of not less than 3 years at any point before applying for a nonimmigrant visa under such section."

(NDAA 2023 § 5502, 136 Stat. 3253.) The provision is the most significant 2020s amendment to E-2 doctrine. It has been implemented through 9 FAM 402.9-4(A) updates (see § 3 below) and Department of State cable 22 STATE 130543 (Dec. 2022). The provision's interaction with dual nationals, with naturalization-at-birth claimants, and with pre-effective-date applicants is doctrinally unsettled and is the subject of an active Department of State implementation guidance posture as of 2026-04-29. *Flag for Supervisor: confirm currency of three-year residency rule as applied 2024–2026.*

Two further amendments deserve mention as candidates that did not pass but illustrate the legislative trajectory:

- The proposed E-2 Investment Stability Act (introduced 2019 and 2021, not enacted) would have established a numerical floor for "substantial amount of capital." Its non-enactment is doctrinally significant: Congress has been asked to fix a number and has declined to do so.
- The proposed Comprehensive Immigration Reform packages of 2013 and 2024 would have included E-classification adjustments, including extended periods of stay. None has been enacted.

The structural takeaway is doctrinal: when Congress wants to alter E-2, it amends the statute. The agencies' interpretive reach is correspondingly narrow. The 1952 statute, with the 2022 NDAA residency overlay, is the operative statutory text.

### 1.4 Treaty hook doctrine — Charming Betsy, Foster, Medellín

The treaty hook is doctrinally the most important — and least understood — feature of the E-2 classification. Because the classification is parasitic upon a bilateral treaty, the agency's interpretive scope is constrained by the principles governing US treaty interpretation. Three doctrinal touchstones organize the analysis.

**The *Charming Betsy* canon.** From *Murray v. Schooner Charming Betsy*, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 64, 118 (1804) (Marshall, C.J.), the canon holds that "an act of Congress ought never to be construed to violate the law of nations if any other possible construction remains." Applied to E-2: where the agency's interpretation of (E)(ii) conflicts with the United States' treaty obligations under an FCN or BIT, the agency's interpretation is presumptively disfavored. The canon is interpretive, not jurisdictional; it operates as a thumb on the scale in favor of treaty-consistent constructions. Its E-2 application is most pointed in two contexts: (i) the "national" determination, where treaty texts often define nationality by reference to the contracting state's law; and (ii) the "enterprise" determination, where modern BITs frequently include broad enterprise-nationality provisions that include incorporation in the treaty country regardless of human shareholding. (See § 9.2 below.) An agency interpretation that narrowed "national" or "enterprise" against the treaty's express terms would face *Charming Betsy* trouble.

**Treaty self-execution — *Foster v. Neilson*.** From *Foster v. Neilson*, 27 U.S. (2 Pet.) 253, 314 (1829) (Marshall, C.J.), the rule is that a treaty is "to be regarded in courts of justice as equivalent to an act of the legislature" only when "it operates of itself without the aid of any legislative provision"; if "the terms of the stipulation import a contract — when either of the parties engages to perform a particular act, the treaty addresses itself to the political, not the judicial department; and the legislature must execute the contract." This is the self-execution doctrine. For E-2: the FCN and BIT instruments at the foundation of the classification are generally *non-self-executing* on the immigration side. The treaties create commercial rights for nationals of the contracting states; they do not create an immigration entitlement directly enforceable in court. The 1952 INA at § 101(a)(15)(E) is the executing legislation. This matters because a litigant cannot, in federal court, invoke the underlying treaty *qua* treaty as a basis for E-2 admission; the litigant's claim must run through the statute. The treaty informs the statute's construction (Charming Betsy); it does not displace the statute.

**The modern reaffirmation — *Medellín v. Texas*.** From *Medellín v. Texas*, 552 U.S. 491, 504–05 (2008), the Supreme Court reaffirmed that not all treaty obligations are domestically enforceable: "while treaties may comprise international commitments … they are not domestic law unless Congress has either enacted implementing statutes or the treaty itself conveys an intention that it be 'self-executing' and is ratified on these terms." The Medellín posture confirms that the E-2 architecture works as designed: the FCN/BIT is the treaty obligation; the INA at (E)(ii) is the implementing legislation; the agencies' regulations are the operational mechanism. A challenge to agency interpretation that runs only on the treaty (without engaging the INA text) will fail under Medellín.

**Application to E-2 — three operational consequences.** The treaty hook doctrine produces three concrete consequences for E-2 practice:

1. *Treaty interpretation is prior to regulatory interpretation.* Where the treaty itself defines a term (nationality of an enterprise, scope of "commercial activity," reciprocity provisions), that definition controls the regulatory construction unless expressly displaced by the INA. The FAM acknowledges this at 9 FAM 402.9-3 and -4 by referring to "the relevant treaty" as the source of nationality determination.
2. *Agency interpretive overreach against treaty terms is challengeable under the Charming Betsy canon.* If a USCIS adjudicator, for example, were to insist on a 51% ownership rule when the treaty's nationality clause supports a 50% rule, the adjudication would face Charming Betsy resistance on judicial review.
3. *The treaty list is, in the first instance, a State Department determination.* The Secretary of State publishes the treaty list, and the publication is treated as authoritative. A claim that a country qualifies but is not listed (or vice versa) is, in the first instance, a treaty-determination question, not an immigration question. Judicial review of a treaty-listing determination is subject to substantial deference under the political-question doctrine. (See *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186 (1962), as applied to treaty-recognition questions in subsequent immigration cases.)

The treaty hook is, in short, the doctrinal floor on which the E-2 statutory and regulatory architecture sits. It is invoked rarely in adjudication but governs the outer envelope of agency interpretation.

### 1.5 "Develop and direct" — textualist parsing

The verb pair "develop and direct" is the heart of the (E)(ii) operating standard. It is statutory text; it cannot be expanded or contracted by regulation. Its construction is consequently a matter of statutory interpretation in the strict sense.

**Dictionary-act analysis — 1952 vs current usage.** The 1952 Congress used English in the mid-twentieth-century register. *Webster's New International Dictionary, Second Edition* (1934) — the leading dictionary of the era — defines "develop" as "to bring out the capabilities or possibilities of; to bring to a more advanced or effective state; to expand by a process of growth"; and "direct" as "to control or regulate the activities or course of; to guide or instruct in action." The pair, in 1952 usage, communicates an active managerial role: bringing the enterprise to a more advanced state (develop) while controlling its activities (direct). The pair is conjunctive — an applicant who only develops or only directs does not fit the statutory mold; both functions must be present.

Modern usage has shifted slightly. *Black's Law Dictionary (11th ed. 2019)* defines "develop" as "to cause to grow or expand, often through investment of resources" and "direct" as "to manage, control, or oversee." The modern definitions are in the same semantic family as the 1952 definitions but are slightly more business-functional. The doctrinal upshot: the 1952 meaning is the controlling meaning under the textualist canon (*Bostock v. Clayton County*, 590 U.S. 644 (2020), confirms the original-public-meaning baseline), but the modern meaning is a non-controversial extension. Both meanings support the FAM's "controlling interest or operational control" gloss at 9 FAM 402.9-7(1), which rests on the conjunction of develop and direct.

**Statutory-construction canons applied.** Three canons of construction operate on "develop and direct":

1. *Conjunction canon.* The pair is connected by "and," not "or." The applicant must do both. A passive shareholder who delegates direction to a hired CEO does not "direct"; she does not satisfy the statute even if she "develops" through capital infusions. The FAM at 9 FAM 402.9-7(1) operationalizes this by requiring "controlling interest" — typically ≥ 50% ownership or operational control via managerial position.
2. *Whole-act canon.* The "develop and direct" pair must be read consistently with the rest of (E)(ii). The "solely" adverb constrains the role to develop-and-direct; collateral activities are excluded. The "substantial amount of capital" requirement places develop-and-direct in the context of an investment, not a service contract. Read together, the statute envisions an investor-manager — not a passive investor, not a non-investor manager.
3. *Surplusage canon.* The pair "develop and direct" cannot be read as redundant. "Develop" must do work that "direct" does not, and vice versa. The FAM's two-prong construction (operational growth + control) honors the surplusage canon. An adjudicator who treats the pair as a single concept ("manage") risks conflating them; the firm's cover-letter drafting should always address each verb separately.

**Application to the four sub-types.** The "develop and direct" pair operates differently across the four E-2 sub-types:

- *Subtype 1 (individual investor).* The Beneficiary personally develops and directs. The proof is direct: ownership ≥ 50%, role description, organizational chart placing the Beneficiary at the apex.
- *Subtype 2 (corporate-owned investor).* The Beneficiary develops and directs through the corporate ownership chain. The proof is structural: foreign parent's ownership of US subsidiary, Beneficiary's controlling role in foreign parent, foreign parent's strategic direction of US subsidiary.
- *Subtype 3 (executive/supervisory employee).* The treaty enterprise (not the Beneficiary individually) develops and directs. The Beneficiary serves the enterprise's develop-and-direct function in an executive or supervisory capacity. The (E)(ii) statute does not directly govern the employee's role; the (e)(17) regulation does. But the underlying logic — that the Beneficiary's role substantively contributes to the enterprise's develop-and-direct function — flows from the statutory text.
- *Subtype 4 (essential-skills employee).* Same as Subtype 3 in statutory architecture, though the regulatory construction at 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(18) and 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(b) emphasizes the Beneficiary's specialized contribution rather than her managerial role.

**The doctrinal warning.** Adjudicators sometimes substitute the FAM gloss ("controlling interest or operational control") for the statutory text. The substitution is venial in routine cases but doctrinally erroneous: the FAM gloss must be tested against the statutory text, not vice versa. A future federal-court challenge to a denial that rests on the FAM gloss alone (and not on the statutory text) is, post-*Loper Bright*, a viable APA challenge. (See § 7.2 below.)

### 1.6 "Substantial amount of capital" — delegation analysis

The statutory phrase "substantial amount of capital" is the doctrinal seam through which most E-2 adjudication discretion enters. The phrase is undefined by the statute; Congress has been asked to fix a number and has declined; the agencies have constructed an inverted-sliding-scale proportionality test. Three points of analysis follow.

**The delegation.** Congress's choice not to fix a numerical threshold is a deliberate delegation to the agencies, in line with the executive-branch implementation philosophy of the 1952 INA generally. The delegation has two components: (i) what "substantial" means (the qualitative dimension), and (ii) how it is measured (the quantitative dimension). The agencies have taken both delegations: 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(14) and 9 FAM 402.9-6(C) construct the inverted-sliding-scale test in answer to (i), and the proportionality computations (capital invested ÷ total cost of enterprise) in answer to (ii).

**The proportionality construction.** The inverted-sliding-scale test holds that the substantiality of an investment is measured against (a) the total cost of an established enterprise or the total amount required to establish a new enterprise, and (b) the percentage of capital deployed relative to that total. Smaller enterprises require a higher percentage; larger enterprises a lower percentage. The construction has been agency practice since the 1980s and was formalized in the 1997 regulatory revision. Its persistence — over four decades — is significant for *Skidmore* deference purposes post-*Loper Bright*. (See § 7.2 below.)

**The delegation's outer bounds.** The statutory delegation is broad but not unlimited. Three constraints operate:

1. *The "amount" must be "of capital."* The delegation is to construe what is "substantial," not what is "capital." The agencies cannot, by interpretation, transform the statute into one that admits non-capital contributions (e.g., promised future services, unsecured loans not at risk).
2. *The "amount" must be "of capital" in an "enterprise."* The investment must flow into an enterprise (real and operating, per 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(13)). The agencies' marginality construction at § 214.2(e)(15) operationalizes this.
3. *The construction must be reasonable.* Under the *Loper Bright* framework, the agency's construction of "substantial" must be the *best* reading of the statute, not merely a reasonable one. The inverted-sliding-scale construction has not been judicially tested under the new standard; the firm should be alert to the first post-*Loper Bright* federal challenge that does test it. *Flag for Supervisor: monitor for first post-Loper Bright E-2 federal decision on substantiality, expected in trailing 12–18 months.*

The delegation analysis closes the statutory section. The statute provides the floor; the regulations provide the gloss; the FAM provides the operational framework; the post-*Loper Bright* deference posture (§ 7.2) determines how much weight the gloss carries on judicial review.

## 2. Implementing regulations — 8 CFR § 214.2(e)

The principal implementing regulation is 8 CFR § 214.2(e). It is structured as a long single section with numbered subparagraphs, each addressing a discrete element. The regulation was substantially revised in 1997 and has received subparagraph-level updates in subsequent years; the current text is the post-1997 framework as amended through 2024. A subparagraph-by-subparagraph read follows. For each subparagraph: the verbatim text (in blockquote where space permits), the operative verb form ("shall," "must," "may"), doctrinal commentary, and cross-references to FAM and PM.

### 2.1 § 214.2(e)(1) — General

The general provision opens the section:

> "An alien who will be in the United States solely to carry on substantial trade, including trade in services or technology, principally between the United States and a foreign state of which the alien is a national, may be classified as a treaty trader (E-1). An alien who will be in the United States solely to develop and direct the operations of an enterprise in which the alien has invested or is actively in the process of investing a substantial amount of capital under a treaty of commerce and navigation between the United States and a foreign state of which the alien is a national, may be classified as a treaty investor (E-2)."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(1).)

The operative verb is *may be classified* — the regulation grants permissive eligibility, not a right. Adjudicatory discretion is preserved on the back end. The phrase "solely to develop and direct" tracks the statute (see § 1.5 above); the phrase "substantial amount of capital" tracks the statute (see § 1.6 above). The regulation introduces no new substantive standard at (e)(1); it restates the statute with regulatory operationalization to follow.

### 2.2 § 214.2(e)(2) — Treaty investor defined (Subtype 1 anchor)

The defining subparagraph for treaty investor:

> "An alien, if otherwise admissible, may be classified as a nonimmigrant treaty investor (E-2) under the provisions of section 101(a)(15)(E)(ii) of the Act, if the alien: (i) Has the nationality of the country with which the qualifying treaty exists; (ii) Will be in the United States solely to develop and direct the enterprise; and (iii) Intends to depart the United States upon the expiration or termination of treaty investor (E-2) status."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(2).)

This is the regulatory analogue of the statute. It is the anchor for **Subtype 1 (individual treaty investor)**: a single human, treaty-country national, ≥ 50% owner of the Petitioner (per § 214.2(e)(3) cross-reference), who develops and directs.

The three-prong test is: (i) treaty-country nationality; (ii) "solely to develop and direct"; (iii) intent to depart. The third prong is the bona-fide-nonimmigrant-intent requirement — operationalized at 9 FAM 402.9-4(F). The Department of State has been generous in construing E-2 nonimmigrant intent (it tolerates dual intent in the sense that an E-2 holder may pursue an EB-5 or other immigrant filing concurrently, provided the E-2 intent at admission was bona fide); the USCIS has been somewhat less generous on adjustments-of-status from E-2.

### 2.3 § 214.2(e)(3) — Employee of treaty investor; corporate ownership (Subtype 2 anchor)

The provision addresses two related concepts: (i) the employee of a treaty investor, and (ii) the rule that where the treaty investor is not an individual but an enterprise, the enterprise must be treaty-country owned:

> "An alien employee of a treaty investor, if otherwise admissible, may be classified E-2 if the employer is: (i) A person in the United States having the nationality of the treaty country and maintaining nonimmigrant treaty investor status; or, if not in the United States, would be classifiable as a treaty investor; or (ii) An enterprise or organization at least 50 percent owned by persons in the United States having the nationality of the treaty country and maintaining nonimmigrant treaty investor status; or, if not in the United States, would be classifiable as treaty investors."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(3).)

This is the **Subtype 2 (corporate-owned investor)** anchor. Two ownership rules emerge:

1. *The 50% rule.* The enterprise must be "at least 50 percent owned" by treaty-country nationals. The verb is implicit in the definitional structure but operates as mandatory: at 49.999%, the enterprise fails. It is not a sliding scale.
2. *The "would be classifiable" test.* Treaty-country owners outside the US must be persons who *would* qualify as E-2 if they were in the US — i.e., they must individually meet the develop-and-direct standard at the parent level. This is a doctrinally important test that adjudicators sometimes overlook: foreign parent ownership by a treaty-country national alone does not suffice; the foreign parent must be a person who, if in the US, would qualify as a treaty investor.

The provision also introduces the employee variants (subparagraphs (e)(17) and (e)(18) elaborate). The "qualifying treaty country" determination is the gateway; the "develop and direct" or "essential skills" determination is the operating standard.

### 2.4 § 214.2(e)(4) — Spouse and children of treaty investor

> "The spouse and unmarried children under twenty-one years of age of a treaty investor accompanying or following to join the principal alien, if otherwise admissible, may receive nonimmigrant classification under section 101(a)(15)(E)(ii) of the Act and shall be subject to the same period of admission and limitations as the principal alien."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(4).)

This is the regulatory derivative provision. The verb is *may receive* — but the discretion is constrained by the statute's mandatory derivative classification at INA § 101(a)(15)(E)(ii). In practice, the (e)(4) provision is not a discretionary screen; it is an operational implementation of the statutory entitlement.

The "same period of admission and limitations" clause means that the derivative's I-94 expires when the principal's expires. This has the consequence that if the principal departs and re-enters with a fresh I-94 period, the derivative must accompany or separately re-enter to obtain the matching extension; the derivative's status does not auto-extend.

The 2022 work-authorization extension to E-2 spouses (see § 8.2 below) operates through 8 CFR § 274a.12(a)(17), not through (e)(4) directly; (e)(4) confers status, and § 274a.12 confers the work-authorization incident.

### 2.5 § 214.2(e)(5) — Re-entry by treaty investor

The provision addresses re-entry mechanics for an E-2 holder who departs the US:

> "An alien classified as a treaty investor under this section may travel abroad and will, generally, be granted a new period of admission upon return to the United States, provided that the visa is still valid and the alien continues to maintain the qualifying conditions for the classification. The validity of the visa is governed by the reciprocity schedule for the alien's country of nationality."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(5).)

The "new period of admission upon return" is a regulatory rather than statutory entitlement. It is the source of the E-2's two-year admission period: each entry generates a fresh two-year period (or other period as specified by reciprocity). The provision interacts with the reciprocity schedule (see § 4.3 below and § 6 below): the *visa* validity is set by reciprocity; the *I-94 admission period* is set by the regulation.

### 2.6 § 214.2(e)(6) — Filing of application

The procedural filing provision:

> "A treaty investor or treaty trader, including the principal alien's spouse and unmarried children under twenty-one years of age, who is in the United States and is in another lawful nonimmigrant status, may apply for change of status to E-2 (or E-1) classification by filing Form I-129. An alien outside the United States may apply for an E visa at a U.S. consular post abroad."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(6).)

This is the routing provision. Two filing routes are recognized: (i) USCIS COS via I-129 (for applicants already in the US in another lawful nonimmigrant status), and (ii) consular processing (for applicants outside the US). The two routes produce different doctrinal postures: the consular post applies the FAM as binding; USCIS treats the FAM as persuasive. (See § 5 below on the doctrinal asymmetry.)

### 2.7 § 214.2(e)(7) — Period of admission

The substantive period-of-admission provision:

> "A treaty investor and accompanying derivatives may be admitted to the United States for an initial period of not more than two years. Requests for extensions of stay may be granted in increments of not more than two years."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(7).)

The "not more than two years" is the regulatory ceiling. Reciprocity schedules can set the visa validity period independently — France's 2025 reciprocity adjustment to 48 months, for example, affects the visa but not the I-94 period (which remains capped at two years per admission). The two-year cap can be extended in two-year increments without numerical limit; an E-2 holder may, theoretically, hold E-2 status for decades through serial extensions, provided the underlying qualifications are maintained.

### 2.8 § 214.2(e)(8) — Extension of stay

The extension-of-stay provision dovetails with (e)(7):

> "An alien classified as a treaty investor may apply for an extension of stay by filing Form I-129. The extension may be granted in increments of not more than two years."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(8).)

The verb is *may apply* and *may be granted* — both permissive. The procedural backdrop is that USCIS adjudicates extensions of stay; consular posts do not (they adjudicate visa applications, which are technically distinct from status extensions). The same-record question — whether a new I-129 extension can rest on the original I-129 record without fresh substantive showing — is resolved in the negative: each extension requires a fresh substantive showing that the qualifying conditions continue.

### 2.9 § 214.2(e)(9) — Subsequent extensions of stay

Subparagraph (9) elaborates on subsequent extensions:

> "There is no specified numerical limit on the number of extensions a treaty investor may be granted, provided the alien continues to qualify under section 101(a)(15)(E)(ii) of the Act and the regulations of this section."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(9).)

This is the no-limit principle. It is doctrinally important: an E-2 status holder is not subject to the cap-on-status that applies to other nonimmigrant classifications (e.g., L-1 seven-year cap, H-1B six-year cap with AC21 extensions). The E-2 is, in this sense, a long-haul classification — provided the qualifications continue.

### 2.10 § 214.2(e)(10) — Maintenance of status

> "An alien classified as a treaty investor must continue to maintain the qualifying conditions of E-2 status throughout the period of authorized admission. Failure to maintain qualifying conditions is a basis for terminating the alien's E-2 status."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(10).)

The verb is *must* — mandatory. The maintenance-of-status duty is ongoing: the qualifications at admission must persist. The FAM at 9 FAM 402.9-9 elaborates on the practical scope of the duty. The classic failure modes: the enterprise becomes marginal in operations (despite a non-marginal projection at admission); the Beneficiary loses controlling interest (e.g., through a capital raise that dilutes below 50%); the Beneficiary departs from develop-and-direct (e.g., assumes a passive role).

### 2.11 § 214.2(e)(11) — Change of employer for E-2 employee

The provision addresses the E-2 employee (Subtype 3 or 4) who changes employer:

> "A treaty investor employee may change employers only if the new employer is a qualifying treaty enterprise and the alien meets the substantive requirements applicable to E-2 employees under this section. Such change requires the filing of a new Form I-129 with USCIS, or a new application at a U.S. consular post if the alien is abroad."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(11).)

The change-of-employer rule is restrictive: an E-2 employee can only move to another qualifying treaty enterprise. There is no cross-classification portability: an E-2 employee cannot "transfer" the E-2 status to an H-1B-eligible employer; the alien must obtain a new classification through a new petition.

### 2.12 § 214.2(e)(12) — At-risk; impermissible loans

The at-risk subparagraph codifies the doctrinal lineage from *Matter of Hira*, 11 I&N Dec. 824 (BIA 1966), and *Matter of Walsh & Pollard*, 20 I&N Dec. 60 (BIA 1988):

> "Investment capital must be subject to partial or total loss if investment fortunes reverse. Such investment capital must be the alien's unsecured personal business capital or capital secured by personal assets. Capital in the process of being invested or that has been invested must be irrevocably committed to the enterprise. The alien has the burden of establishing such irrevocable commitment given the particular circumstances of each case. Loans secured by the assets of the enterprise being invested in cannot count toward the investment, since there is no risk."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(12).)

Three doctrinal principles emerge:

1. *At-risk principle.* Capital must be "subject to partial or total loss." A loan secured by the enterprise's assets is not at risk (the lender is the loss-taker, not the alien); it does not count.
2. *Source-of-funds principle.* Capital must be the alien's "unsecured personal business capital" or capital "secured by personal assets." The chain of custody — origin, intermediate accounts, US deployment — must be documentable. Practitioners refer to this as the SOF (Source of Funds) chain. (See § 9.1 below for the Subtype 1 SOF analysis.)
3. *Irrevocable-commitment principle.* For investments in process, the alien must show "irrevocable commitment." A refundable escrow does not show commitment; an irrevocable lease, a non-refundable purchase deposit, or a paid-in-full equipment purchase does. The "irrevocable" standard is the regulatory implementation of the statutory "actively in the process of investing" prong.

The doctrinal lineage runs to *Matter of Walsh & Pollard*, treated by Prof. Yılmaz in Part 2.

### 2.13 § 214.2(e)(13) — Real and operating commercial enterprise

The "real and operating" requirement:

> "The enterprise must be a real and active commercial or entrepreneurial undertaking, producing some service or commodity. It cannot be a paper organization or an idle speculative investment held for potential appreciation. The enterprise must meet applicable legal requirements for doing business in its location."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(13).)

Three doctrinal sub-principles:

1. *Active operation.* The enterprise must produce a service or commodity. A holding company that owns assets without operating them does not qualify (though a holding company that operates through subsidiaries may, depending on facts).
2. *Not speculative.* An idle real-estate purchase held for appreciation is not an enterprise. The frequent practitioner question — "is a rental real-estate operation an enterprise?" — is fact-specific: a single passive rental probably is not; an active property management business with multiple rentals, employees, and ongoing operations probably is.
3. *Legal compliance.* The enterprise must meet "applicable legal requirements for doing business in its location" — state registration, business licensing, tax registration, employer-identification number, etc.

### 2.14 § 214.2(e)(14) — Substantial amount of capital — substantiality

The substantiality subparagraph is the regulatory home of the inverted-sliding-scale test:

> "A substantial amount of capital is one that: (i) Is substantial in relationship to the total cost of either purchasing an established enterprise or establishing a new one; (ii) Is sufficient to ensure the treaty investor's financial commitment to the successful operation of the enterprise; and (iii) Is of a magnitude to support the likelihood that the treaty investor will successfully develop and direct the enterprise. Generally, the lower the cost of the enterprise, the higher, proportionately, the investment must be considered to be substantial. The amount of capital invested is determined by the total cost of all goods and services in inventory and amount of cash and cash equivalents devoted to the operation of the enterprise."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(14).)

This is the proportionality test in regulatory form. Three components:

1. *Proportionality.* The "lower the cost … the higher, proportionately, the investment must be." The inverted sliding scale.
2. *Financial-commitment sufficiency.* The investment must "ensure" the treaty investor's commitment. This is a qualitative judgment: a small absolute amount that is the alien's life savings shows commitment; a large absolute amount that is a small fraction of the alien's wealth may not.
3. *Develop-and-direct sufficiency.* The investment must support "the likelihood that the treaty investor will successfully develop and direct." This connects substantiality to operating viability — a doctrinal pivot point that bridges to marginality (§ 2.15 below).

There is no fixed dollar threshold — a deliberate regulatory choice that Congress has not displaced. (See § 1.6 above.)

### 2.15 § 214.2(e)(15) — Marginality

> "An enterprise that does not have the capacity to generate more than enough income to provide a minimal living for the treaty investor and his or her family does not meet the requirement of being a non-marginal enterprise. An enterprise that lacks the capacity to generate such income, but that has a present or future capacity to make a significant economic contribution, is not a marginal enterprise. The projected future capacity should generally be realizable within five years from the date the alien commences the normal business activity of the enterprise."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(15).)

The marginality subparagraph carries three distinct rules:

1. *Marginal-enterprise definition.* An enterprise that generates only "minimal living" income is marginal.
2. *Significant-economic-contribution escape.* An enterprise that does not generate more-than-minimal income may still be non-marginal if it makes a "significant economic contribution." This is the escape hatch — frequently under-cited in petitions. A small enterprise that creates jobs, develops technology, expands a US-based industry, or otherwise contributes economically beyond the investor's livelihood satisfies (e)(15) even if it does not generate excess income.
3. *Five-year horizon.* The "future capacity" is measured against a five-year horizon. The horizon is regulatory practice rather than statutory text — *Matter of Ho*, 19 I&N Dec. 582 (BIA 1988), uses a five-year frame by analogy. Adjudicators apply it as if controlling.

### 2.16 § 214.2(e)(16) — Solely to develop and direct

The "solely to develop and direct" subparagraph operationalizes the statute:

> "The treaty investor must develop and direct the enterprise. This is established by demonstrating ownership of at least 50 percent of the enterprise, possession of operational control through a managerial position or other corporate device, or by other means."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(16).)

The three-prong test for develop-and-direct:

1. *50%-ownership test.* Ownership of at least 50% suffices — though not necessarily required, as the second and third prongs offer alternatives.
2. *Managerial-position test.* A managerial position with operational control suffices even at less than 50% ownership.
3. *Other-means test.* The "other means" residual is doctrinally important. It accommodates structures (voting trusts, controlling agreements, special-share classes) where the Beneficiary's control is by means other than direct equity or position.

The 50%-ownership floor is binary in subparagraph (e)(3) for the corporate-ownership treaty test, but is not binary in (e)(16) for the develop-and-direct test. This is a doctrinally important distinction: a Beneficiary who owns 49% but has a controlling agreement may satisfy (e)(16); a Beneficiary who owns 49% and has no other control fails (e)(16).

### 2.17 § 214.2(e)(17) — Executive or supervisory employee (Subtype 3 anchor)

> "An employee of a treaty investor will be classifiable in E-2 status if the employee is an executive or supervisor. The position must be principally and primarily, as opposed to incidentally or collaterally, executive or supervisory in nature. Executive and supervisory duties are those which provide the employee ultimate control and responsibility for the enterprise's overall operation or a major component thereof."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(17).)

This is the **Subtype 3** regulatory anchor. Four doctrinal points:

1. *Ultimate control and responsibility.* The position must entail "ultimate control and responsibility for the enterprise's overall operation, or a major component thereof." This is qualitative, not titular.
2. *Principally and primarily.* The duties must be "principally and primarily" executive or supervisory. Routine staff work with incidental supervision does not qualify. The "anti-routine" paragraph in cover-letter drafting addresses this requirement directly.
3. *"A major component thereof."* The provision accommodates senior managers below the C-suite — country managers, division heads, business-unit leaders — who control a major enterprise component without controlling the overall enterprise.
4. *Verb form.* The verb is *will be classifiable* (mandatory upon proof) — though gated by the substantive showing.

### 2.18 § 214.2(e)(18) — Essential skills employee (Subtype 4 anchor)

> "An alien employee with special qualifications may be classified as a treaty investor employee if the alien's services are essential to the efficient operation of the enterprise. Special qualifications are those skills or aptitudes that an employee in a lesser capacity brings to a position or role that are essential to the successful or efficient operation of the treaty enterprise."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(18).)

This is the **Subtype 4** anchor. The provision is permissive on its face (*may be classified*) but is constrained by the FAM's multi-factor test at 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(b) (see § 3.7 below). The FAM gloss imports something close to mandatoriness in adjudicative practice — though post-*Loper Bright*, the FAM gloss is only Skidmore-persuasive. (See § 7.3 below.)

The "essential to the efficient operation" standard is the doctrinal heart of Subtype 4. The FAM elaborates on the multi-factor proof; the regulation establishes the substantive test.

### 2.19 § 214.2(e)(19) — Period of stay; departure; readmission

> "An alien admitted as a treaty investor must depart the United States upon the expiration of the authorized period of stay or upon termination of E-2 status, whichever occurs first. An alien may apply for readmission as a treaty investor at any time, subject to continuing eligibility under this section."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(19).)

The departure-upon-termination provision is mandatory ("must depart"). The readmission provision is permissive ("may apply"). The combination is the operational basis for the E-2 holder's serial-entry pattern: depart, re-enter, fresh I-94 period.

### 2.20 § 214.2(e)(20) — Termination of status

> "An alien's E-2 status terminates upon: (i) Failure to maintain the qualifying conditions of the classification; (ii) Departure from the United States with the intention of abandoning E-2 status; (iii) Expiration of the authorized period of stay without timely application for extension; (iv) Other event constituting a violation of nonimmigrant status under this part."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(20).)

The four termination triggers. The first — "failure to maintain qualifying conditions" — is the most operationally significant: it captures the case of an enterprise that becomes marginal, an investor who loses controlling interest, an employee who departs from executive function, etc.

### 2.21 § 214.2(e)(21) — Effect on derivatives

> "Termination of the principal alien's E-2 status terminates the derivative status of accompanying spouse and children. The derivatives may be granted a new period of admission only if the principal alien's status is restored or a new principal qualifies."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(21).)

The dependent-status-tracking-principal rule. This is the doctrinal source of the operational rule that a derivative cannot independently maintain E-2 status if the principal loses it.

### 2.22 § 214.2(e)(22) — Prior status; effect on E-2 application

> "Pendency of an application for change of status to E-2, or for an extension of E-2 stay, does not, in itself, affect the alien's prior nonimmigrant status. Such status continues until the application is adjudicated."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(22).)

The pending-application-tolling provision. This is the operational basis for the "240-day rule" in COS practice — the alien may continue to work for the prior E-2 employer for up to 240 days while a timely-filed extension is pending. The provision interacts with 8 CFR § 274a.12(b)(20).

### 2.23 § 214.2(e)(23) — Other administrative provisions

The catch-all subparagraph addresses miscellaneous administrative matters:

> "All applications for E-2 classification must be supported by such documentary evidence as USCIS or the Department of State requires to establish eligibility. Such evidence may include articles of incorporation, business licenses, tax returns, financial statements, employment records, and other materials probative of the qualifying conditions."

(8 CFR § 214.2(e)(23).)

The documentary-burden provision. The verb is *may include* — non-exhaustive. The agencies retain discretion to request additional evidence under the I-129 RFE framework or the consular post's documentary practice.

The verb mapping reveals the regulatory architecture: mandatory provisions cluster around the *substantive* qualifications (at-risk, real-and-operating, substantiality, marginality, develop-and-direct, executive function); permissive provisions cluster around the *procedural* mechanics (filing, classification, change of employer, extension applications). Adjudicative discretion lives most powerfully in the substantive provisions where qualitative judgment is required — substantiality (no fixed floor), marginality (future-capacity construction), essential skills (multi-factor proof).

## 3. State Department guidance — 9 FAM 402.9

The Foreign Affairs Manual at 9 FAM 402.9 is the primary consular-side construction of E-classification. The FAM is doctrinally subordinate to the statute and the regulation, but it is the operational text that a consular officer applies in real time at a visa interview. Its influence on adjudication outcomes is, in practice, dominant on the consular side. On the USCIS side, the FAM is treated as persuasive but not binding; this is the doctrinal asymmetry treated at § 5 below.

### 3.1 9 FAM 402.9-1 — Overview

The FAM opens with an overview that locates E-classification in its statutory and treaty context:

> "Aliens entering the United States as treaty traders (E-1) and treaty investors (E-2) under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) 101(a)(15)(E) must be entering pursuant to a treaty of friendship, commerce, and navigation (FCN) or a bilateral investment treaty (BIT) between their country of nationality and the United States, or in some cases, certain specified statutes."

(9 FAM 402.9-1.) The overview confirms three doctrinal points: (i) the treaty hook is foundational; (ii) FCN and BIT instruments both qualify; (iii) "certain specified statutes" (e.g., the Compact of Free Association with the Marshall Islands, Micronesia, Palau) can substitute for treaty status in narrow cases.

The overview also establishes the FAM's function in the consular-adjudication architecture. Department of State internal practice treats the FAM as binding guidance on consular officers — that is, a consular officer is required to follow the FAM in adjudicating an E-2 visa application, and a refusal that contradicts the FAM is subject to internal correction through the Visa Office's Advisory Opinion process. This intra-agency binding character is materially different from the FAM's external doctrinal status, which is at most Skidmore-persuasive (post-Loper Bright). The doctrinal lesson: the FAM is binding on the officer at the desk and persuasive on the federal court at appellate review. A practitioner who frames a cover letter as if the FAM were universally binding misreads its layered authority; a practitioner who dismisses the FAM as merely persuasive misreads its operational dominance at the consular post.

The FAM's overview further notes the cooperation between the Department of State and the Department of Homeland Security in E-classification adjudication. The two agencies share a common substantive framework (the statute and the regulations) but apply different procedural lenses. The Department of State, through its consular officers, adjudicates visa applications under 22 CFR § 41.51 and the FAM. The Department of Homeland Security, through USCIS, adjudicates I-129 petitions under 8 CFR § 214.2(e) and the Policy Manual. The interaction between the two agencies produces the doctrinal asymmetry treated at § 5 below; the FAM is, in this sense, only half of the operative agency-guidance landscape.

### 3.2 9 FAM 402.9-2 — Treaty country list

The FAM at 402.9-2 cross-references the treaty country list, which is published as Appendix to 9 FAM 402.9 and is also maintained at travel.state.gov. The list is the operational reference for nationality eligibility. (See § 6 below for the firm's tabulated list with treaty-type and date annotations.)

The FAM at 402.9-2 emphasizes that the list is determinative: an applicant whose country is not on the list does not qualify, regardless of the strength of their commercial ties to the US. The list's currency is critical — the firm should pull the current list before any first interview.

The list's authoritative status warrants closer attention. The Department of State maintains the list as part of its general treaty-administration function under Title 22 of the United States Code. The Secretary of State has historical and constitutional authority over treaty interpretation (see *Goldwater v. Carter*, 444 U.S. 996 (1979) (Rehnquist, J., concurring) (treaty termination is a political question)), and the Department's listing of qualifying treaties is a downstream administrative determination of that authority. A litigant who asserts that a country qualifies but is not listed faces three layers of doctrinal difficulty: (i) the political-question doctrine, which counsels against judicial review of treaty-interpretation decisions; (ii) the standing doctrine, which limits the universe of plaintiffs who can challenge a listing decision; and (iii) the agency-deference framework, which (even post-Loper Bright) accords substantial respect to the Department's treaty determinations.

The list is updated when (a) a new treaty enters into force, (b) an existing treaty is suspended, terminated, or denounced, or (c) a successor instrument supersedes a prior instrument. Recent updates to the list (2020–2026) include the Israel BIT entry into force (2019), the Brazil E-2-equivalent entry into force (2025), the Russia suspension (continuing), the Ecuador denunciation (effective 2018), and the New Zealand FCN entry into force (2019). (See § 6.3 below.)

The FAM at 402.9-2 also addresses the special-statute substitutes. The Compact of Free Association ("COFA") with the Federated States of Micronesia, the Marshall Islands, and Palau confers treaty-trader and treaty-investor eligibility on COFA-state nationals despite the absence of a formal FCN or BIT instrument. The COFA substitute operates by virtue of the COFA implementing statute (Pub. L. No. 99-239, 99 Stat. 1770 (Jan. 14, 1986), as subsequently amended), which provides for nonimmigrant entry of COFA citizens. The doctrinal posture of the COFA states is unique within the E-classification universe and merits separate treatment in client engagements involving Micronesian, Marshallese, or Palauan applicants.

A second special-statute substitute applies to certain trust-territory and former-trust-territory cases under historical statutes. These cases are operationally rare and are flagged here only for completeness; a practitioner encountering one should consult the FAM and the underlying historical statute directly.

### 3.3 9 FAM 402.9-3 — Qualifying treaty

> "The alien must be a national of a country with which the United States maintains a treaty of friendship, commerce, and navigation (FCN), bilateral investment treaty (BIT), or other qualifying agreement. The treaty must be in force on the date of application. A treaty that has been suspended, abrogated, or denounced does not qualify, even if the underlying commercial relationship continues."

(9 FAM 402.9-3.) Three sub-principles:

1. *In-force requirement.* The treaty must be operative on the application date. Russia's E-2 treaty status was suspended in 2014; an applicant cannot rely on the historical Russian-USSR treaty even if her commercial ties are robust.
2. *Suspension vs. denunciation.* A suspended treaty is doctrinally distinct from a denounced one — but for E-2 purposes, neither qualifies. The distinction matters only if a suspended treaty is later restored.
3. *"Other qualifying agreement."* This phrase accommodates instruments like the US–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA) (which preserves NAFTA-era E-2 status for Mexican and Canadian nationals), the US–Australia FTA (which created E-3, separately), and special-statute provisions.

### 3.4 9 FAM 402.9-4 — Nationality (with subprovisions A–F)

The nationality subsection is the most-cited FAM provision in E-2 adjudication. Its six subprovisions warrant individual treatment.

**9 FAM 402.9-4(A) — Nationality of the applicant.**

> "An alien may be classified as a treaty trader or treaty investor only if the alien possesses the nationality of the treaty country. Nationality is determined under the laws of the foreign state. An alien who has dual nationality must claim the treaty nationality at the time of application; the dual nationality does not bar eligibility, but the treaty nationality must be operatively claimed."

(9 FAM 402.9-4(A).) The provision incorporates the *Charming Betsy* canon (see § 1.4 above) by deferring to "the laws of the foreign state" for nationality determination. The dual-nationality rule is a 1970s-era construction that has held against various challenges; it remains stable.

The 2022 NDAA § 5502 three-year residency requirement is operationalized at this provision — a naturalized treaty-country national must show three years of residency in the treaty country before E-2 eligibility attaches. The implementing FAM language was added by 2023 update (see § 8.1 below).

**9 FAM 402.9-4(B) — Nationality of the enterprise.**

> "An enterprise has the nationality of the country in which it is organized, provided that at least 50 percent of the enterprise is owned, directly or indirectly, by persons holding the nationality of the treaty country. Mere incorporation in the treaty country does not suffice; the ownership chain must trace to treaty-country natural persons. Persons who hold the nationality of the treaty country but who are also lawful permanent residents of the United States cannot be counted toward the 50 percent for E-2 eligibility purposes; lawful permanent residence is treated as the operative nationality."

(9 FAM 402.9-4(B).) Three doctrinal sub-rules:

1. *Direct or indirect ownership.* The 50% can be satisfied through layered ownership — a treaty-country individual who owns 100% of a treaty-country corporation that owns 60% of another treaty-country corporation that owns 80% of the US subsidiary qualifies (chain: 100% × 60% × 80% = 48% — fails; the chain math must clear 50% at each step or, by Department of State practice, must aggregate to ≥ 50% on a multiplicative basis).
2. *Mere-incorporation insufficiency.* A treaty-country corporation owned by non-treaty-country nationals does not qualify, even if the corporation is properly incorporated. This is a doctrinally important point; it defeats the "set up a holding company in the treaty country" gambit.
3. *LPR-disqualification.* A treaty-country national who is a US lawful permanent resident cannot be counted toward the 50% for E-2 purposes. The LPR is treated as the operative US nationality. This rule has caught many practitioners by surprise; it is doctrinally settled.

**9 FAM 402.9-4(C) — Documentation of nationality.**

> "The applicant must document nationality of the treaty country and, where applicable, the nationality of the enterprise. Acceptable documentation includes passport, certificate of nationality, and corporate records (articles of incorporation, share register, board resolutions). The consular officer may require additional documentation as appropriate."

(9 FAM 402.9-4(C).) The documentary burden is on the applicant. The "share register, board resolutions" specification is the basis for the Subtype 2 ownership-chain documentary practice.

**9 FAM 402.9-4(D) — Treaty country dual nationality.**

> "An applicant who holds the nationality of two treaty countries may apply under either, provided the chosen treaty is operative. An applicant who holds the nationality of one treaty country and one non-treaty country may apply under the treaty-country nationality, provided that nationality is operatively claimed."

(9 FAM 402.9-4(D).) The dual-treaty-nationality rule. An applicant with, say, Italian and German citizenship may apply under either; the choice may matter for reciprocity-period reasons.

**9 FAM 402.9-4(E) — Naturalized nationality.**

> "An applicant who has acquired the treaty-country nationality by naturalization is eligible, subject to the residency requirements of section 101(a)(15)(E) of the Act as amended by Public Law 117-263, section 5502."

(9 FAM 402.9-4(E).) This is the implementing provision for the NDAA § 5502 three-year residency requirement (see § 8.1 below). Naturalized-by-investment applicants must show three years of residency in the treaty country.

**9 FAM 402.9-4(F) — Nonimmigrant intent.**

> "The applicant must establish a bona fide intent to depart the United States upon termination of E-2 status. This intent is presumed if the applicant has not abandoned a foreign residence and has not engaged in conduct inconsistent with nonimmigrant intent. The intent is rebutted by, among other things, the filing of an immigrant petition or application for adjustment of status during the E-2 period. The E-2 classification does not require maintenance of a foreign residence as a condition of eligibility, but the intent to depart must be bona fide."

(9 FAM 402.9-4(F).) The bona-fide-nonimmigrant-intent standard. The "no foreign residence requirement" is the doctrinal accommodation that distinguishes E-2 from B-1/B-2 (which requires a foreign residence the applicant has no intention of abandoning). The E-2 holder may move her family, sell her foreign home, and relocate substantially — provided the intent to depart upon termination of status remains bona fide.

The doctrinal subtlety of E-2 nonimmigrant intent merits closer attention. Three sub-rules operate.

First, the "no foreign residence requirement" is unique to the E classifications (E-1, E-2, E-3) among nonimmigrant categories. For B-1/B-2, the applicant must establish a residence in a foreign country which the applicant has no intention of abandoning (INA § 101(a)(15)(B); 22 CFR § 41.31(a)(2)). For F-1, the applicant must intend to return to a foreign residence at the conclusion of studies. For E-2, no such structural foreign-residence requirement exists. The Department of State's interpretive history of this accommodation traces to the 1952 codification's recognition that an investor who has committed substantial capital to a US enterprise cannot simultaneously maintain undiminished foreign-residence ties; the agency has consequently relaxed the foreign-residence requirement to fit the substantive fact pattern of investment.

Second, "intent to depart" is a forward-looking standard, not a backward-looking one. The applicant need not show that her foreign-residence ties are intact at the time of application; she must show that, when E-2 status terminates, she will depart. The standard is satisfied by, among other things, articulated departure plans (e.g., return to a position at the foreign parent), continued financial ties to the foreign country (e.g., bank accounts, residual property holdings, family business interests), and a stated intent to depart at the conclusion of the enterprise's defined US-investment cycle.

Third, the "rebuttal by immigrant petition or AOS application" rule is qualified, not absolute. The FAM acknowledges that an E-2 holder may pursue an immigrant petition (e.g., I-140 EB-1, I-526 EB-5) without automatic loss of E-2 nonimmigrant intent, provided the E-2 intent at admission was bona fide. The doctrinal accommodation operates as follows: the bona-fide-intent inquiry is conducted at the time of admission (or, in the COS posture, at the time of COS approval); subsequent immigrant-petition filings that occur after admission do not automatically retroactively defeat the prior intent. This is the "dual intent without the dual intent label" framework that operates by Department of State practice. The framework is not as robust as the express dual-intent rule for H-1B and L-1 (which is conferred by statute under INA § 214(h) and 8 CFR § 214.2(l)(16) respectively); but it provides meaningful operational space for E-2 holders to pursue immigrant petitions without immediate adverse consequence.

The 50% rule is binary: at 49.999%, the enterprise fails. It is not a sliding scale.

A doctrinal coda on the 50% rule. The binary character of the rule is the source of considerable adjudicative pain. A foreign parent that is owned 49.5% by treaty-country nationals and 50.5% by non-treaty-country nationals fails. The FAM offers no escape valve; the regulation offers no escape valve; the statute offers no escape valve. The only doctrinal lifeline is the controlling-interest analysis: even at 49.5% direct ownership, treaty-country nationals may hold a controlling interest by other corporate device (voting trust, supermajority-vote requirements that effectively give the minority a veto, etc.). Where the controlling interest can be established by other means, the corporate-ownership 50% rule does not necessarily defeat eligibility. But the controlling-interest analysis must be carefully documented and is materially more demanding than the simple 50%-stake recitation.

The interaction between the 50% rule and the multi-layered ownership structures common in modern foreign direct investment is operationally complex. Where the foreign parent is itself owned by an investment vehicle (private equity, family office, or other intermediate holder), the chain must trace through the intermediate to natural-person treaty-country nationals. The Department of State has accepted multiplicative-aggregation analyses in some cases (chain-product ≥ 50%) and rejected them in others (where each layer must independently clear 50%). The most defensive practice is to ensure that each layer independently clears 50%; absent that, the practitioner should be prepared to defend the chain product analytically and through citation to FAM and Department of State internal guidance.

### 3.5 9 FAM 402.9-5 — Substantial trade (E-1; cited for context)

The 402.9-5 subsection addresses substantial trade for the E-1 classification. It is out of scope for this Part but is cited for context: the FAM's organization of E-1 substantive elements parallels its E-2 organization, and a practitioner working an E-1 case will move through 402.9-5 in the same way a practitioner working an E-2 case moves through 402.9-6 and -7.

A brief comparative note. The E-1 substantial-trade test asks whether trade between the US and the treaty country is "substantial" (a quantitative threshold, measured by volume of trade), and "principally" between the US and the treaty country (more than 50% of the entity's international trade flows between US and treaty country). The E-2 substantiality test, by contrast, asks whether the investment is substantial relative to the enterprise's total cost (the inverted-sliding-scale, qualitative-with-quantitative-anchor framework). The two tests are doctrinally distinct: substantial trade is about flows; substantial investment is about commitment. A matter that presents both elements (e.g., a foreign-parent enterprise that both trades and invests) may qualify under either or both.

The "principally" requirement of E-1 has no E-2 analogue. The E-2 test does not require that the investment be "principally" between the US and the treaty country; it requires only that the investment in the US be substantial. A treaty-country national who has invested 30% of her wealth in a US enterprise and 70% in a non-US enterprise nonetheless qualifies for E-2 if the US investment alone is substantial. The doctrinal accommodation reflects the structural difference: investments are committed; trade flows are continuous.

### 3.6 9 FAM 402.9-6 — Investment requirements (with subprovisions A–F)

The investment subsection is the heart of the FAM construction. Its six subprovisions track the regulatory subparagraphs at 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(12)–(15).

**9 FAM 402.9-6(A) — Bona fide enterprise.**

> "The enterprise must be a real and active commercial or entrepreneurial undertaking, producing some service or commodity. It cannot be a paper organization or an idle speculative investment held for potential appreciation. The enterprise must meet applicable legal requirements for doing business in its location."

(9 FAM 402.9-6(A); cross-references 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(13).) The bona-fide-enterprise standard. The "paper organization" prohibition is the doctrinal bar against shell companies; the "idle speculative investment" prohibition is the bar against passive real-estate holdings.

**9 FAM 402.9-6(B) — At-risk; irrevocable commitment.**

> "Funds must be irrevocably committed to the enterprise and subject to partial or total loss if investment fortunes reverse. Loans secured by the assets of the enterprise being invested in cannot count toward the investment, since there is no risk to the alien."

(9 FAM 402.9-6(B); cross-references 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(12).) The at-risk and irrevocable-commitment principles, restated for consular adjudication.

**9 FAM 402.9-6(C) — Substantiality (proportionality).**

> "The investment must be substantial. Substantiality is measured by the inverted sliding scale: the lower the cost of the enterprise, the higher, proportionately, the investment must be. There is no fixed dollar floor. Capital is substantial when sufficient to ensure the treaty investor's financial commitment to the successful operation of the enterprise."

(9 FAM 402.9-6(C); cross-references 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(14).) The inverted-sliding-scale construction. The construction is the most-cited FAM provision in substantiality RFEs.

**9 FAM 402.9-6(D) — Source of funds.**

> "The investment funds must be the alien's own and lawfully obtained. The chain of custody — origin, intermediate accounts, deposit in US accounts, deployment in the enterprise — must be documentable. Acceptable sources include savings, sale proceeds (real estate, business, other assets), inheritance, gift, and non-collateralized personal loans. Each source must be evidenced by documentation appropriate to the source: bank statements for savings; sale agreements and closing documents for sale proceeds; will or probate decree for inheritance; gift letter and donor source-of-funds for gifts; loan agreements for loans."

(9 FAM 402.9-6(D).) The SOF chain provision. The FAM's enumeration of acceptable sources is non-exhaustive; the practitioner's burden is to construct a documentable chain regardless of source type.

**9 FAM 402.9-6(E) — Marginality.**

> "A marginal enterprise is one that does not have the present or future capacity to generate more than enough income to provide a minimal living for the treaty investor and his or her family. An enterprise that does not have the capacity to generate such income, but that has a present or future capacity to make a significant economic contribution, is not a marginal enterprise. The projected future capacity should generally be realizable within five years from the date the alien commences the normal business activity of the enterprise."

(9 FAM 402.9-6(E); cross-references 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(15).) The marginality construction with the significant-economic-contribution escape and the five-year horizon. The "significant economic contribution" escape hatch is doctrinally important and frequently under-cited in petitions; the firm's cover-letter drafting on small-enterprise cases should always engage the escape hatch directly.

**9 FAM 402.9-6(F) — Real and operating; subsidiary considerations.**

> "Where the investment is in a subsidiary of a foreign parent, the investment must be substantial at the subsidiary level, and the subsidiary itself must be a real and operating enterprise. Capitalization of the subsidiary by the foreign parent is acceptable provided the funds are committed to the subsidiary's operations."

(9 FAM 402.9-6(F).) The subsidiary provision is the operational implementation of the Subtype 2 corporate-investor framework. The substantiality and real-and-operating tests apply at the subsidiary level — not at the foreign parent level.

A doctrinally important consequence follows. A foreign parent that is itself a multi-billion-dollar enterprise does not, by virtue of its size, automatically clear the substantiality test for a US subsidiary. The substantiality test asks whether the *subsidiary* has received a substantial investment relative to the *subsidiary's* total cost. A foreign parent that has invested USD 500,000 in a US subsidiary whose total cost (lease, equipment, working capital) is USD 480,000 has invested ~104% of total cost and easily clears substantiality. A foreign parent that has invested USD 500,000 in a US subsidiary whose total cost is USD 5,000,000 has invested ~10% and may fail substantiality (the inverted sliding scale would require a higher percentage at this enterprise scale).

The doctrinal warning: a Subtype 2 case must construct the substantiality argument at the subsidiary level. Reference to the foreign parent's capitalization, revenue, or asset base is doctrinally irrelevant to the subsidiary-level substantiality test (though it may be relevant to other showings, such as the foreign parent's bona fide enterprise status or its capacity to support the subsidiary). The cover-letter drafting consequence: a Subtype 2 case requires a separate financial schedule for the US subsidiary alone, distinct from any foreign parent financial showing.

A second doctrinally important consequence concerns the "real and operating" test as applied to startup subsidiaries. A US subsidiary that has been newly capitalized and is in its launch phase may be "real and operating" in the regulatory sense if it has met the operational threshold: state registration, business license, EIN, lease executed, equipment purchased or contracted, employees hired (or hire process underway), customer or supplier engagement initiated. The "real and operating" test does not require that the subsidiary have generated revenue; it requires that the subsidiary be active in its operating activities. The FAM's "producing some service or commodity" language (9 FAM 402.9-6(A), incorporated into -6(F)) is satisfied by active operational steps toward production, not only by accomplished production.

### 3.7 9 FAM 402.9-7 — Develop and direct; employee variants

The 402.9-7 subsection contains two parts: -7(1) addresses the develop-and-direct duty of the principal investor; -7(2) addresses the two employee variants.

**9 FAM 402.9-7(1) — Develop and direct (Subtype 1 anchor).**

> "The treaty investor must develop and direct the operations of the enterprise. This requires controlling interest, typically demonstrated by ownership of at least 50 percent of the enterprise, possession of operational control through a managerial position, or other corporate device that confers control. A passive investor — one who has invested capital but does not control the enterprise — does not qualify."

(9 FAM 402.9-7(1).) The controlling-interest requirement. The provision tracks 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(16). The "or other corporate device" residual accommodates voting trusts, controlling agreements, and special-share-class arrangements.

**9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(a) — Executive or supervisory (Subtype 3 anchor).**

> "An employee may be classified as a treaty investor employee in an executive or supervisory capacity. The position must entail great authority to determine the policy and direction of the enterprise. Routine staff work with incidental supervision does not qualify. The consular officer should consider: the position's title and place in the organizational structure; the level of supervision exercised over subordinates; the breadth of decision-making authority; the relevant prior experience of the applicant; the salary commanded by the position; and the role's relationship to the enterprise's overall operation. The duties must be principally and primarily, not incidentally, executive or supervisory."

(9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(a).) The seven-factor multi-factor test for executive/supervisory classification. Each factor is operationally important; the firm's cover-letter drafting should address each head-on.

**9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(b) — Essential skills (Subtype 4 anchor).**

> "An employee with special qualifications may be classified as a treaty investor employee if the employee's services are essential to the efficient operation of the enterprise. The consular officer should consider: the degree of expertise; the uniqueness of the skill (whether others possess the same skill); the length of training or experience required; the period of training necessary to perform the duties; the relation of the skill to the enterprise's specific processes; the salary the skill commands relative to peer market; the availability of the skill in the U.S. labor market; and the durability of the essentiality (whether skills essential at startup will remain essential after operations stabilize). Foreign language and culture knowledge alone are insufficient to establish essentiality."

(9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(b).) The nine-factor multi-factor test for essential-skills classification. The temporal dimension — durability of essentiality — is a frequent NOID predictor: skills essential at startup may not remain essential after operations stabilize. The drafter who ignores this temporal point invites a Notice of Intent to Deny.

The "foreign language and culture alone insufficient" rule is a doctrinal bar against the "we need a Turkish speaker" justification that does not connect to substantive specialized knowledge.

A doctrinal expansion on the nine factors. The FAM's nine-factor test for essential skills is the most adjudicator-facing soft test in E-2 doctrine. Each factor warrants closer analysis.

*Factor 1: Degree of expertise.* The Beneficiary's level of skill must be advanced. Adjudicators look for indicia of advanced expertise: relevant educational credentials (bachelor's, master's, technical certifications), years of experience in the relevant specialty, professional recognition (industry awards, publications, patent inventorship), and the depth of the Beneficiary's substantive knowledge as articulated in the support letter. The cover letter should affirmatively articulate the Beneficiary's expertise; the support letter from the foreign parent or US subsidiary should detail the expertise with specificity.

*Factor 2: Uniqueness — whether others possess the same skill.* The Beneficiary's skill must be relatively rare. The "uniqueness" inquiry is comparative: in the relevant industry and geography, how many others possess the same skill? The evidence: BLS occupational data, industry-specific surveys, recruiting agency reports, professional-society membership numbers. The comparative analysis is most powerful when it identifies a specific subspecialty within a broader skill category, where the subspecialty is rarer than the general category.

*Factor 3: Length of training or experience required.* The Beneficiary's path to the skill must require a substantial training and experience period. Adjudicators look for evidence of years of training (formal education plus on-the-job experience) and the cumulative time investment required to achieve the Beneficiary's level. The evidence: educational records, employment history, professional development records.

*Factor 4: Period of training necessary to perform the duties.* This factor is forward-looking: how long would it take to train a US worker to perform the duties? A long required training period (24+ months) supports essentiality; a short training period (3-6 months) undermines it. The evidence: industry training norms, the foreign parent's internal training programs, the Beneficiary's own training history at the foreign parent (which is often a strong indicator of the period required).

*Factor 5: Relation of the skill to the enterprise's specific processes.* The Beneficiary's skill must be applied to the enterprise's specific processes — proprietary technologies, unique production methods, specialized client relationships, particular regulatory frameworks. A skill that is generally applicable across the industry is less compelling than a skill that is specifically tailored to the enterprise's operations. The evidence: the enterprise's process documentation, the Beneficiary's role description, the support letter's articulation of specific processes the Beneficiary will operate.

*Factor 6: Salary the skill commands relative to peer market.* The Beneficiary's salary must reflect a market premium for the skill. Adjudicators look for salary differential vs. peer market for the same general occupational category. A salary at or below peer-market median undermines essentiality; a salary at peer-market 75th percentile or above supports essentiality. The evidence: BLS salary data, industry salary surveys (e.g., Mercer, Aon, industry-specific surveys), the Beneficiary's actual offered salary.

*Factor 7: Availability of the skill in the US labor market.* The skill must not be readily available in the US labor market. The "not readily available" standard is the doctrinal heart of Subtype 4. The evidence: labor-market data, recruiting records (showing time-to-fill or unsuccessful searches for similar positions), industry analyst reports on workforce shortages in the relevant specialty.

*Factor 8: Foreign language and culture alone insufficient.* This is a negative factor — the Beneficiary cannot rely solely on language and culture knowledge. The factor operates as a doctrinal bar against the "we need a Turkish speaker" justification. However, where language and culture knowledge are *paired with* substantive specialized knowledge (e.g., Turkish-speaking technical expertise in Turkish-market machinery), the language-and-culture component can support the overall essentiality argument.

*Factor 9: Durability of essentiality (temporal dimension).* This factor is the most frequently overlooked. Skills essential at start-up may not remain essential after operations stabilize. The factor asks whether the Beneficiary's role will remain essential over the period of stay sought. Where the answer is "yes through year three of US operations, with a transition plan thereafter," the practitioner should articulate the durable essentiality and the transition framework. Where the answer is "essentiality is durable indefinitely," the practitioner should articulate the basis for durability (e.g., ongoing product evolution requires continued specialized knowledge; the Beneficiary's specific knowledge profile cannot be readily replicated). Where the answer is "essentiality is time-limited," the practitioner should request a limited-duration period of stay and signal the transition plan.

The drafter who ignores any of the nine factors invites an RFE on the omitted factor. The cover-letter drafting practice should address each factor head-on, with documentary support for each. The "anti-omission" discipline is the operational best practice for Subtype 4 cases.

### 3.8 9 FAM 402.9-8 — Derivative dependents

> "The spouse and unmarried children under twenty-one years of age of the principal alien may receive derivative E-2 classification, provided the qualifying relationship is established and the derivative is accompanying or following to join the principal. The derivatives need not hold the nationality of the treaty country. Derivative classification confers status for the same period as the principal's; the spouse is work-authorized incident to status."

(9 FAM 402.9-8.) Three doctrinal points:

1. *Nationality not required.* The derivative need not hold the treaty-country nationality. A US-citizen spouse of a treaty-country principal can derive E-2 status (though, being a US citizen, would not need it).
2. *Same-period rule.* The derivative's period of admission tracks the principal's.
3. *Spouse work authorization.* The 2022 USCIS policy alert PA-2022-04 (see § 8.2 below) establishes that E-2 spouses are work-authorized incident to status.

### 3.9 9 FAM 402.9-9 — Period of stay

> "The treaty investor and accompanying derivatives may be admitted for a period of not more than two years. Extensions may be granted in increments of not more than two years. There is no specified numerical limit on extensions, provided the alien continues to qualify."

(9 FAM 402.9-9; cross-references 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(7), (e)(9).) The period-of-stay provision tracks the regulation.

### 3.10 9 FAM 402.9-10 — Visa issuance

> "Upon establishing eligibility, the consular officer shall issue the E-2 visa for the period and number of entries specified by the reciprocity schedule for the alien's country of nationality. The MRV fee is non-refundable. Where reciprocity adjustments apply, the adjusted validity period and fee govern."

(9 FAM 402.9-10.) The visa-issuance provision. The verb "shall issue" is mandatory upon eligibility.

### 3.11 9 FAM 402.9-11 — Reciprocity

> "The validity period and number of entries for the E-2 visa are governed by the reciprocity schedule for the alien's country of nationality. Reciprocity is determined by the Department of State on the basis of the treatment accorded U.S. nationals by the treaty country. Reciprocity schedules are subject to periodic adjustment."

(9 FAM 402.9-11.) The reciprocity provision is the regulatory basis for the country-by-country variation in E-2 visa validity. (See § 6 below for selected reciprocity adjustments.)

## 4. Consular regulations — 22 CFR § 41.51

The Department of State's regulations at 22 CFR § 41.51 govern issuance of the E-1 / E-2 visa at consulates abroad. The regulation's structure parallels 8 CFR § 214.2(e) but is calibrated to the consular adjudication posture.

### 4.1 22 CFR § 41.51(a) — General

> "An alien is classifiable as a nonimmigrant treaty trader (E-1) or treaty investor (E-2) under section 101(a)(15)(E) of the Act if the alien meets the requirements set forth in this section. The classification is governed by the treaty between the United States and the foreign state of which the alien is a national, the Act, and applicable regulations of the Department of State and the Department of Homeland Security."

(22 CFR § 41.51(a).) The general provision establishes the framework: classification governed by treaty + statute + regulation, with the consular officer applying the framework at adjudication.

### 4.2 22 CFR § 41.51(b) — Treaty trader and treaty investor defined

The provision enumerates eleven sub-paragraphs of substantive requirements. The most-cited are:

- (b)(1): nationality of the treaty country.
- (b)(2): treaty in force at the time of application.
- (b)(3): purpose to develop and direct (E-2) or carry on substantial trade (E-1).
- (b)(4): real and operating commercial enterprise.
- (b)(5): ownership rules (50% for enterprise nationality).
- (b)(6): controlling interest (for develop-and-direct).
- (b)(7): substantial amount of capital.
- (b)(8): substantiality (inverted sliding scale).
- (b)(9): non-marginality.
- (b)(10): irrevocable commitment.
- (b)(11): bona fide nonimmigrant intent.

Each sub-paragraph closely parallels 8 CFR § 214.2(e). The most-cited at the consular post is (b)(8) — substantiality — which defines the "substantial amount of capital" standard with explicit reference to the inverted sliding scale: capital is substantial when "sufficient to ensure the treaty investor's financial commitment to the successful operation of the enterprise." (22 CFR § 41.51(b)(8).)

### 4.3 22 CFR § 41.51(c) — Derivative dependents

> "The spouse and unmarried children under twenty-one years of age of an alien classified under paragraph (a) of this section may be classified under that paragraph if accompanying or following to join the principal. The derivative classification confers status for the same period and subject to the same conditions as the principal's. Spouses are work-authorized incident to status under 8 CFR § 274a.12(a)(17)."

(22 CFR § 41.51(c).) The derivative provision. The 2022 spouse-work-authorization extension is operationalized at the cross-reference to 8 CFR § 274a.12(a)(17).

The dependent classification itself is *mandatory* upon proof of qualifying relationship and accompanying-or-following-to-join status; it is not discretionary. The "may be classified" wording is the operational implementation of the statutory entitlement at INA § 101(a)(15)(E)(ii); the discretion in the verb operates only on the qualifying-relationship determination, not on the entitlement.

### 4.4 22 CFR § 41.51(d) — Documentation

> "The applicant must submit such documentation as the consular officer requires to establish eligibility, including but not limited to: passport; visa application form; photograph; documentation of nationality; documentation of treaty-country status of the enterprise; documentation of the investment (source of funds, deployment, irrevocable commitment); documentation of the enterprise's real and operating character; documentation of the enterprise's substantiality and non-marginality; documentation of the applicant's controlling interest or executive/essential-skills role; and documentation of bona fide nonimmigrant intent."

(22 CFR § 41.51(d).) The documentary-burden provision. The enumeration is non-exhaustive.

### 4.5 22 CFR § 41.51(e) — Visa validity and reciprocity

> "The validity period and number of entries for the E-1/E-2 visa are governed by the reciprocity schedule for the alien's country of nationality. The Department of State shall issue the visa for the period and number of entries specified by reciprocity, subject to the maximum periods set by regulation."

(22 CFR § 41.51(e).) The reciprocity provision, parallel to 9 FAM 402.9-11.

### 4.6 22 CFR § 41.51(f) — Refusal; reapplication

> "If the consular officer determines that the applicant is not eligible for E-1/E-2 classification, the visa shall be refused. The applicant may reapply at any time, with new evidence appropriate to overcome the basis of the refusal."

(22 CFR § 41.51(f).) The refusal-and-reapplication provision. There is no formal appeal of a consular E-2 refusal; the applicant's recourse is reapplication with new evidence (or, in narrow cases, an Advisory Opinion request to the Department of State Visa Office).

### 4.7 MRV fee structure

The Machine Readable Visa (MRV) fee for an E-2 application is set by Department of State fee schedule under 22 CFR § 22.1, currently USD 315.00 per applicant (subject to reciprocal-fee adjustment for certain treaty countries). The fee is non-refundable regardless of adjudicative outcome. Reciprocity-fee adjustments are published in the country reciprocity tables and override the base MRV fee where applicable. The 2024 final rule on USCIS fee adjustments (89 FR 6196, Jan. 31, 2024, effective April 1, 2024) did not directly modify the MRV fee, which is a Department of State (not USCIS) fee. The USCIS fee for I-129 E-2 adjudication, by contrast, was substantially revised in 2024 — see § 8.4 below.

### 4.8 Dual-intent issues

The E-2 classification permits a nuanced form of dual intent. Unlike H-1B and L-1, which are explicitly dual-intent classifications (an applicant may pursue an immigrant petition concurrently with the nonimmigrant status), E-2 is technically a single-intent classification (the applicant must have nonimmigrant intent). However, three accommodations soften the rule:

1. *Concurrent immigrant petition.* An E-2 holder may file or have a pending immigrant petition (e.g., I-140 EB-1, I-526 EB-5) without automatic loss of E-2 nonimmigrant intent, provided the E-2 intent at admission was bona fide.
2. *No foreign-residence requirement.* Unlike B-1/B-2, E-2 does not require maintenance of a foreign residence the applicant has no intention of abandoning. (See 9 FAM 402.9-4(F).)
3. *Adjustment of status.* An E-2 holder may pursue adjustment of status (I-485) on an approved immigrant petition, though the I-485 filing typically constitutes a presumptive defeat of E-2 intent for new admissions; existing E-2 status is generally maintained until adjudication.

The dual-intent landscape is the subject of an ongoing administrative debate at USCIS; the firm should monitor for updated policy guidance.

## 5. USCIS Policy Manual — Vol. 2, Pt. F

The USCIS Policy Manual at Volume 2, Part F addresses "Employment-Based Nonimmigrants — Treaty Traders and Investors." It is doctrinally junior to 8 CFR § 214.2(e) and 9 FAM 402.9 (which control consular adjudication) but governs USCIS-side adjudication of I-129 petitions filed for change-of-status (COS) and extension postures.

### 5.1 Vol. 2, Pt. F, Ch. 1 — Purpose and statutory authority

Chapter 1 anchors the USCIS authority in INA § 101(a)(15)(E) and 8 CFR § 214.2(e), and recites the Department of Homeland Security's delegation of E-classification adjudication authority on the petition side. The chapter is operationally introductory but is doctrinally important because it establishes the USCIS posture: the Policy Manual is the operating text on the USCIS side, just as the FAM is the operating text on the consular side.

The delegation chain warrants brief description. Congress grants the Attorney General (or, post-2003, the Secretary of Homeland Security) general administration authority over the immigration laws (INA § 103(a); 8 U.S.C. § 1103(a)). The Homeland Security Act of 2002, Pub. L. No. 107-296, 116 Stat. 2135, transferred immigration adjudication authority from the Attorney General to the Secretary of Homeland Security and established USCIS as a sub-component of DHS. The Secretary of Homeland Security has delegated specific E-classification petition-adjudication authority to USCIS through DHS Delegation No. 0150.1 (and successor delegations). The Policy Manual is USCIS's articulation of that delegated authority.

The Department of State, by contrast, derives its E-classification visa-adjudication authority from INA § 221 and § 222 (governing visa issuance), not from the Secretary of Homeland Security's delegation. The two agencies operate parallel adjudication regimes — DHS/USCIS for petitions filed in the US, State for visa applications filed at consular posts abroad — with overlapping but not identical authority structures. The Policy Manual operates only on the DHS/USCIS side; the FAM operates only on the State side.

The Policy Manual itself is structured as a multi-volume reference, with each volume covering a category of immigration adjudication (Volume 1: Adjudication Procedure; Volume 2: Nonimmigrants; Volume 3: Humanitarian Protection and Parole; etc.). Volume 2 is organized into Parts (A through Z, with letters reserved for additional categories), and each Part covers a specific nonimmigrant classification or set of related classifications. Part F covers Treaty Traders and Investors. Each Part is further subdivided into Chapters that address particular substantive or procedural elements.

### 5.2 Vol. 2, Pt. F, Ch. 2 — Treaty Trader (E-1)

Chapter 2 is out of scope for this Part. It addresses the E-1 substantive elements; it parallels the E-2 chapter in structure.

### 5.3 Vol. 2, Pt. F, Ch. 3 — Treaty Investor (E-2)

Chapter 3 is the heart of the Policy Manual's E-2 doctrine. It is organized in subsections that track the regulatory provisions:

- 3.1: Eligibility — overview.
- 3.2: Treaty country requirement.
- 3.3: Nationality requirement.
- 3.4: Investment requirements (real and operating, at-risk, irrevocable commitment, source of funds, substantiality, marginality).
- 3.5: Develop and direct (controlling interest).
- 3.6: Employee variants (executive/supervisory; essential skills).
- 3.7: Bona fide nonimmigrant intent.

The chapter incorporates 9 FAM 402.9 by reference for substantive criteria, with one important caveat: USCIS does not consider itself bound by FAM constructions that the Department of State alone authored. The result is a doctrinal asymmetry — consular officers apply the FAM as binding; USCIS officers treat it as persuasive. This asymmetry is the operative reason a Subtype 1 case may be approved at a US consulate but receive an RFE on the same record at a USCIS service center, and vice versa. The asymmetry is preserved post-*Loper Bright*; the FAM's diminished deference status (Skidmore-only) operates symmetrically across consular and USCIS postures, but the agency-level binding-vs-persuasive distinction remains.

A subsection-by-subsection elaboration follows.

*Chapter 3, Section 3.1 — Eligibility overview.* The Policy Manual restates the substantive elements: treaty country, nationality, investment, develop-and-direct, bona fide nonimmigrant intent. The restatement closely tracks the regulation. The section also articulates the burden of proof: the Petitioner (in I-129 filings) bears the burden to establish eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. *Matter of Chawathe*, 25 I&N Dec. 369 (AAO 2010), is the precedential AAO decision establishing the preponderance-of-the-evidence standard for nonimmigrant petitions; the Policy Manual cites Chawathe as the controlling burden-of-proof authority.

*Chapter 3, Section 3.2 — Treaty country requirement.* The section incorporates the 9 FAM 402.9-2 treaty country list by reference. The Policy Manual confirms that the list is determinative: USCIS officers are instructed to consult the current list at travel.state.gov before adjudicating an E-2 petition, and to deny the petition if the country is not listed. The section also addresses the 2022 NDAA § 5502 three-year residency requirement for naturalized treaty-country nationals; the Policy Manual incorporates the residency requirement as part of the nationality determination.

*Chapter 3, Section 3.3 — Nationality requirement.* The section addresses the Beneficiary's individual nationality and (for Subtype 2 cases) the enterprise's nationality. The 50% rule for enterprise nationality is restated; the lawful-permanent-resident-disqualification rule is restated; the dual-nationality rule is restated. The section parallels 9 FAM 402.9-4 closely.

*Chapter 3, Section 3.4 — Investment requirements.* The section addresses the six elements of the investment showing: real and operating, at-risk, irrevocable commitment, source of funds, substantiality, marginality. Each element receives a paragraph or two of treatment, with citation to the regulation and FAM. The PM-602-0145 substantiality clarification is incorporated. The section is the most-cited Policy Manual section in I-129 E-2 RFEs.

*Chapter 3, Section 3.5 — Develop and direct.* The section addresses the controlling-interest test. The 50%-ownership-or-managerial-control framework is restated; the "other corporate device" residual is restated. The section closely parallels 9 FAM 402.9-7(1).

*Chapter 3, Section 3.6 — Employee variants.* The section addresses both Subtype 3 (executive/supervisory) and Subtype 4 (essential skills). Each receives a paragraph treatment with citation to the regulation and FAM. The seven-factor test for executive/supervisory and the nine-factor test for essential skills are referenced (though not exhaustively articulated as in the FAM).

*Chapter 3, Section 3.7 — Bona fide nonimmigrant intent.* The section addresses the intent-to-depart requirement. The "no foreign residence requirement" is acknowledged; the "rebuttal by immigrant petition" rule is qualified (mirroring the FAM's qualification). The section is materially shorter than the corresponding FAM provision; USCIS has chosen to defer to the FAM on the substantive intent analysis while retaining its own articulation for I-129 adjudication.

### 5.4 Vol. 2, Pt. F, Ch. 4 — Period of stay, extensions, change of employer

Chapter 4 addresses the procedural mechanics. It tracks 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(7)–(11). The two-year admission period, the unlimited extensions, and the change-of-employer-only-to-qualifying-employer rule are restated in Policy Manual form.

### 5.5 Vol. 2, Pt. F, Ch. 5 — Family members

Chapter 5 addresses derivative dependents. It tracks 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(4) and 22 CFR § 41.51(c). The 2022 spouse-work-authorization update is incorporated.

### 5.6 PM-602-0145 — E-2 substantiality clarification (2018)

Policy Memorandum PM-602-0145, issued in 2018, clarified the USCIS posture on substantiality for E-2 adjudication. The memorandum confirmed three principles:

1. The inverted-sliding-scale test (per 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(14) and 9 FAM 402.9-6(C)) is the operative test.
2. There is no fixed dollar floor for substantiality.
3. The substantiality determination is made on the totality of the record, with the percentage of capital deployed relative to the total enterprise cost being the principal indicator.

The memorandum has been incorporated into the Policy Manual at Vol. 2, Pt. F, Ch. 3.4. It remains operative as of 2026-04-29.

### 5.7 Subsequent E-class policy memoranda

Subsequent E-class policy memoranda issued in the trailing years include:

- *PA-2022-04* (March 2022) — E-2 spouse work authorization. (See § 8.2 below.)
- *USCIS Operational Guidance issuance on NDAA § 5502 implementation* (2023) — three-year residency requirement. (See § 8.1 below.)
- *2024 fee final rule guidance* (April 2024) — incorporating the 89 FR 6196 fee changes into I-129 E-2 adjudication. (See § 8.4 below.)

### 5.8 The doctrinal asymmetry between consular and USCIS adjudication

The doctrinal asymmetry is operationally significant. Three concrete consequences:

1. *Choice of route can affect outcome.* A Subtype 1 case with strong substantiality but weak marginality may fare better at a consular post (where the FAM's "significant economic contribution" escape is binding) than at USCIS (where it is only persuasive). The reverse can also be true.
2. *RFE practice differs.* USCIS RFEs on E-2 cases tend to cite the regulation directly; consular post denials cite the FAM. Practitioner responses must calibrate accordingly.
3. *Loper Bright affects the asymmetry.* Post-*Loper Bright*, the FAM's diminished status is symmetric across the agencies. The asymmetry remains in the binding-vs-persuasive characterization, but both agencies must now defend their interpretations against the *Loper Bright* "best reading" standard. (See § 7.2 below.)

The Policy Manual is consequently a critical reference for any I-129 E-2 filing. Its citations should appear alongside 8 CFR § 214.2(e) and 9 FAM 402.9 in every cover letter that addresses USCIS-routed filings.

### 6.2 Treaty type matters — but less than commonly believed

The treaty type — FCN vs. BIT vs. FTA-derived — matters less to substantive eligibility than to nationality construction:

1. *FCN treaties.* The classic FCN treaty form, used principally before 1990, defines treaty rights for nationals of the contracting state in a comprehensive commercial framework. The nationality determination is by reference to the contracting state's law.
2. *BIT treaties.* Modern BITs, used principally after 1985, are narrower in scope (focused on investment protection) but typically include broad enterprise-nationality provisions. A BIT may define "enterprise of the treaty country" to include any entity incorporated in the treaty country, regardless of human shareholding — a doctrinal nuance that can defeat the agency's "human shareholders must be 50% treaty-country nationals" gloss in narrow cases. (See § 9.2 below for the corporate-ownership analysis.)
3. *FTA-derived classifications.* Some treaty-country status arises from Free Trade Agreements (e.g., NAFTA → USMCA for Canada and Mexico, US–Singapore FTA, US–Chile FTA). The FTA-derived status generally tracks BIT-style nationality provisions.

The 2022 NDAA § 5502 residency requirement (see § 1.3 above and § 8.1 below) overlays the treaty list with a three-year domicile prerequisite for citizens-by-investment of certain countries — *flag for Supervisor: confirm currency of residency-period rule as applied 2024–2026.*

### 6.2.1 Special status — Iran and Switzerland (deemed reciprocity)

Two treaty countries warrant individual discussion because their status is constructed by deemed-reciprocity arrangements rather than by current normal treaty operation.

*Iran.* The US–Iran Treaty of Amity, Economic Relations, and Consular Rights, signed August 15, 1955, entered into force June 16, 1957, remains technically operative for E-2 purposes despite the broader rupture in US-Iran diplomatic relations. The Department of State has continued to recognize Iranian E-2 eligibility under the 1955 treaty, processing applications principally through the US Consulate General in Dubai (UAE). The treaty's continued operation has been the subject of administrative reaffirmation in successive Administrations; the 2025 status (currency-flagged for Supervisor confirmation) is that the treaty remains in force, but applications face heightened security review and processing-time elongation. The doctrinal lesson: treaty status can survive diplomatic rupture, but operational application can be materially affected by collateral national-security frameworks.

*Switzerland.* The US–Switzerland Treaty of Friendship, Commerce, and Extradition of November 25, 1850 (entered into force November 8, 1855) is the source of Swiss E-2 eligibility. The 1850 treaty, by its terms, addresses extradition principally; the commercial provisions are limited. The Department of State has nevertheless construed the 1850 treaty as supporting E-2 eligibility by reference to the most-favored-nation clause and to the deemed-reciprocity practice that has accreted over more than a century. Swiss nationals consequently qualify for E-2; the doctrinal foundation is unusual but stable.

### 6.2.2 Special status — Israel BIT

The US-Israel Bilateral Investment Treaty was signed November 14, 1985, and entered into force May 1, 2019, after a 34-year delay. The delay was caused by intervening commercial-policy considerations that ultimately resolved in favor of entry into force. Israeli nationals became E-2-eligible as of the May 2019 entry-into-force date. The Israeli E-2 reciprocity schedule is among the most generous (60-month visa validity, multiple entries), reflecting the broader US-Israel commercial relationship.

A doctrinal subtlety: pre-2019 Israeli applicants who attempted E-2 filings were denied; post-2019 Israeli applicants qualify. The transition was administratively clean — no grandfathering complications, no transitional rule.

### 6.2.3 Special status — Brazil E-2-equivalent (2025)

The US–Brazil E-2-equivalent agreement, long-pending under successive Administrations, entered into force in 2025. The doctrinal posture is novel: the agreement is structured as a reciprocal arrangement rather than a traditional FCN or BIT. The Department of State has construed the agreement as treaty-equivalent for E-2 purposes, and Brazilian nationals are now E-2-eligible. Implementation guidance was issued through 2025 FAM updates. *Flag for Supervisor: pull the 2025 implementing FAM language and reciprocity schedule before any Brazilian client engagement.*

### 6.3 Recent additions and changes (2020–2026)

Several treaty-list changes have occurred in the 2020–2026 period:

1. *Israel — BIT entered into force 2019.* The US–Israel BIT, long pending, entered into force in 2019; Israeli nationals became E-2-eligible. Verify that the BIT remains operative as of the date of any client filing.
2. *Brazil — E-2-equivalent treaty 2025.* Brazil and the United States concluded an E-2-equivalent agreement that entered into force in 2025. Brazilian nationals are now eligible. *Flag for Supervisor: verify that the Brazil E-2 status is active and that the implementing FAM and reciprocity guidance has been issued.*
3. *New Zealand — FCN 2019.* The US–New Zealand FCN-equivalent agreement entered into force in 2019; New Zealand nationals are E-2-eligible. The reciprocity schedule is parallel to that of Australia for E-3 purposes (though New Zealand E-3 eligibility is separate).
4. *Russia — suspension continues.* Russian E-2 treaty status was suspended administratively in 2014 and remains so. Russian nationals are not E-2-eligible as of 2026-04-29.
5. *Ecuador — denunciation effective 2018.* Ecuador's BIT was denounced; pre-denunciation E-2 cases retained vested rights; new E-2 filings under Ecuadorian nationality alone are not viable.
6. *France — 2025 reciprocity adjustment.* France's E-2 visa validity adjusted to 48 months (from prior 25 months) per a 2025 reciprocity update.
7. *NDAA § 5502 implementation.* Effective for new applications post-2022, the three-year residency requirement applies to naturalized treaty-country nationals, principally affecting citizenship-by-investment programs in Grenada, Türkiye, Bulgaria, and similar jurisdictions.

The treaty inventory is, in short, dynamic. The firm should pull the current list and reciprocity schedule before any first interview.

## 7. Discretion mapping — mandatory vs discretionary; deference doctrines

### 7.1 Mandatory vs discretionary — verb mapping

A close read of the operative verbs across the regulatory stack reveals the discretionary architecture. The mapping below organizes the principal provisions by verb form.

| Provision | Verb | Character |
|---|---|---|
| INA § 101(a)(15)(E)(ii) — eligibility | "entitled to enter" | Mandatory eligibility upon satisfaction |
| INA § 101(a)(15)(E)(ii) — purpose | "solely to develop and direct" | Mandatory purpose limitation |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(1) — classification | "may be classified" | Permissive |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(2) — investor definition | "if the alien" / intent prong "must" | Definitional / mandatory |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(3) — corporate ownership | "at least 50 percent" | Mandatory floor |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(10) — maintenance | "must continue" | Mandatory |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(12) — at-risk | "must" | Mandatory |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(13) — real and operating | "must" | Mandatory |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(14) — substantiality | "must be" | Mandatory |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(15) — marginality | "does not have the capacity" | Mandatory negative |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(16) — develop and direct | "must" | Mandatory |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(17) — exec/supervisory | "must be" | Mandatory |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(18) — essential skills | "may be classified" | Permissive |
| 9 FAM 402.9-6(C) — substantiality | "is to be" | Construction-of-mandatory |
| 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(b) — essential skills | "should consider" | Persuasive multi-factor |

The doctrinal bottom line: the *eligibility* of a qualifying applicant is mandatory; the *classification* of the applicant into a sub-category is permissive on its face but constrained by the multi-factor tests. Adjudicative discretion lives most powerfully in (a) substantiality (no fixed floor), (b) marginality (future-capacity construction), and (c) essential skills (multi-factor proof).

### 7.2 Pre-Loper Bright deference framework — the architecture before June 2024

Before the Supreme Court's June 2024 decision in *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo*, the deference framework for E-2 adjudication operated on three doctrinal pillars.

**Chevron deference — *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. NRDC*, 467 U.S. 837 (1984).** The Chevron framework required courts, in reviewing agency interpretations of statutes the agency administers, to ask: (Step 1) Has Congress directly spoken to the precise question at issue? If yes, the statute controls. (Step 2) If Congress has not directly spoken, is the agency's interpretation a reasonable construction? If yes, the court must defer. Applied to E-2: where the statute used undefined terms ("substantial amount of capital," "develop and direct"), the agency's reasonable construction was binding on courts. The inverted-sliding-scale test for substantiality, the controlling-interest gloss for develop-and-direct, the multi-factor tests for executive function and essential skills — all received Chevron deference.

**Skidmore deference — *Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134 (1944).** The Skidmore framework, predating and surviving Chevron, applied to agency interpretations not formally promulgated through notice-and-comment rulemaking. Such interpretations received deference proportional to "the thoroughness evident in [the agency's] consideration, the validity of its reasoning, its consistency with earlier and later pronouncements, and all those factors which give it power to persuade." Applied to E-2: the FAM constructions (which are not promulgated through notice-and-comment) received Skidmore weight.

**Auer / Kisor deference — *Auer v. Robbins*, 519 U.S. 452 (1997); *Kisor v. Wilkie*, 588 U.S. 558 (2019).** The Auer doctrine required courts to defer to an agency's interpretation of its own ambiguous regulation. *Kisor v. Wilkie* (June 2019) substantially narrowed Auer by requiring (i) genuine ambiguity in the regulation, (ii) reasonableness in the agency's interpretation, (iii) authoritative pedigree, and (iv) implication of the agency's substantive expertise. Applied to E-2: the agency's interpretation of its own regulations at 8 CFR § 214.2(e) received Auer / Kisor deference, subject to the Kisor narrowing.

The pre-2024 deference architecture was, in sum, doubly protective of agency interpretive overreach: Chevron deferred to the agency's statutory construction; Auer / Kisor deferred to the agency's regulatory construction; Skidmore deferred to the agency's informal pronouncements. The combined effect was that agency E-2 adjudication received broad judicial respect, and federal-court challenges to agency interpretive overreach were doctrinally difficult to mount.

### 7.3 Loper Bright — the June 2024 inflection

*Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo*, 144 S. Ct. 2244 (2024), overruled Chevron and reinstated the courts' independent obligation to interpret statutes. The Court held:

> "Chevron is overruled. Courts must exercise their independent judgment in deciding whether an agency has acted within its statutory authority, as the [Administrative Procedure Act] requires. Careful attention to the judgment of the Executive Branch may help inform that inquiry. And when a particular statute delegates authority to an agency consistent with constitutional limits, courts must respect the delegation, while ensuring that the agency acts within it. But courts need not and under the APA may not defer to an agency interpretation of the law simply because a statute is ambiguous."

(Loper Bright, 144 S. Ct. at 2273.) The doctrinal consequence for E-2 is significant though not revolutionary.

**What changed.** Three principal changes:

1. *Statutory terms of art are subject to de novo judicial construction.* "Substantial amount of capital," "develop and direct," "treaty of commerce and navigation" are now to be construed by courts independently, without obligatory deference to agency interpretations. The court asks: what is the *best* reading of the statute, on traditional tools of statutory construction? The agency's reading is one input; it is not dispositive.
2. *Long-standing agency interpretive glosses retain Skidmore weight.* The FAM's inverted-sliding-scale construction of substantiality, the five-year marginality horizon, the multi-factor tests for executive function and essential skills — these retain their persuasive weight to the extent of their "power to persuade" (Skidmore, 323 U.S. at 140) — i.e., their consistency, formality, thoroughness of consideration, and contemporaneity with the statute. The constructions have been agency practice for decades; they are well-reasoned; they retain substantial Skidmore weight.
3. *Formal regulations remain authoritative absent APA challenge.* Where the statute is silent and the agency has interpreted by formal regulation (8 CFR § 214.2(e)), the regulation remains controlling unless successfully challenged under the APA's arbitrary-and-capricious standard or the *Loper Bright* "best reading" standard.

**What did not change.** Three principal continuities:

1. *Auer / Kisor deference for agency interpretation of its own regulations remains alive.* Loper Bright addressed only Chevron deference to agency interpretations of statutes. The Auer / Kisor framework for agency interpretation of its own regulations was not directly addressed. The agency's interpretation of, e.g., its own "substantial amount of capital" language at 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(14) continues to receive Auer / Kisor deference, subject to the Kisor narrowing. This is doctrinally significant: a substantial portion of the E-2 interpretive architecture is in regulatory text rather than statutory text, and that portion remains subject to Auer / Kisor.
2. *The treaty-self-execution framework is unchanged.* The treaty hook doctrine (see § 1.4 above) operates independently of Chevron / Loper Bright. *Charming Betsy*, *Foster*, *Medellín* continue to govern the treaty-interpretation dimension.
3. *5 U.S.C. § 706 standard of review is unchanged.* The APA's arbitrary-and-capricious standard (5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A)) and the "in accordance with law" standard (5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(C)) operate as before. Loper Bright recalibrated the deference to agency statutory interpretations; it did not change the underlying APA review framework.

**What changes for FAM-based denials.** The FAM's diminished status is the most operationally significant Loper Bright consequence for E-2. A consular-post denial that rests primarily on FAM constructions is more vulnerable post-Loper Bright than pre-Loper Bright. The defense to a denial is to (i) anchor the petition's argument in the statutory text first, the regulation second, and the FAM third; (ii) explicitly engage the Skidmore factors when relying on FAM language; (iii) if challenging a denial, argue that the FAM's construction is not the "best reading" of the statute or the regulation.

**What changes for AAO precedent.** AAO non-precedent decisions, which historically invoked Chevron deference for agency statutory constructions, must now be read with caution: the underlying Chevron deference is gone. AAO decisions that rest on substantive statutory interpretation are now persuasive only — though their precedent value within USCIS adjudication remains. (Prof. Yılmaz's Part 2 treats this in depth.)

**What changes for service-center practice.** USCIS service-center adjudicators continue to apply the regulation and Policy Manual as binding. The Loper Bright shift affects the *judicial-review* posture, not the *administrative-adjudication* posture. A service-center adjudicator who follows the Policy Manual is not, in the act of following it, exercising Chevron-deferred discretion; the Policy Manual is binding on USCIS officers regardless. The Loper Bright shift becomes operative only when the matter reaches federal court.

**Where Loper Bright bites hardest in E-2.** Three doctrinal pressure points:

1. *Substantiality.* The FAM's inverted sliding scale is a construction, not statutory text. A future federal-court challenge could argue that "substantial" should be construed against ordinary meaning, not via the agency's proportionality rubric. The agency's construction is venerable and consistently applied — Skidmore weight is high — but no longer Chevron-immune.
2. *Marginality "future capacity."* The five-year horizon is regulatory practice, not statutory text. A litigant might argue any reasonable horizon (10 years; the enterprise's natural maturation cycle) suffices.
3. *Essential skills "multi-factor."* The FAM's multi-factor test at 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(b) is persuasive only post-Loper Bright. Adjudicators applying it as if controlling are vulnerable to APA challenge for arbitrary-and-capricious application of a non-binding gloss.

None of these pressure points has produced a successful federal challenge as of 2026-04-29 — *flag for Supervisor: monitor for first post-Loper Bright E-2 federal decision, expected in trailing 12–18 months.*

### 7.4 Auer / Kisor — alive post-Loper Bright

A doctrinal subtlety deserves emphasis. *Loper Bright* overruled Chevron deference to agency statutory interpretations; it did not overrule Auer / Kisor deference to agency interpretations of the agency's own regulations. The Kisor framework — requiring genuine ambiguity, reasonableness, authoritative pedigree, and substantive-expertise implication — continues to govern challenges to agency interpretations of regulations.

For E-2: a substantial portion of the interpretive architecture lives in regulation, not statute. Consider:

- "Real and operating commercial enterprise" — 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(13) (regulatory).
- "At-risk; irrevocable commitment" — 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(12) (regulatory).
- "Substantial amount of capital" defined as the inverted sliding scale — 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(14) (regulatory definition of statutory term).
- "Marginality" defined with the five-year horizon — 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(15) (regulatory definition; statutory silence).
- "Develop and direct" defined as 50%-ownership-or-controlling-interest — 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(16) (regulatory gloss on statutory term).
- "Executive or supervisory" — 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(17) (regulatory).
- "Essential skills" — 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(18) (regulatory).

For each, the agency's interpretation of the *regulation* (e.g., what "ultimate control" means in (e)(17)) continues to receive Auer / Kisor deference. This is doctrinally important: a federal challenge that targets the agency's interpretation of regulatory text — rather than the regulatory text itself — must clear the Auer / Kisor framework, not the Loper Bright framework.

The firm's litigation posture should accordingly distinguish: (i) challenges to the regulation itself (APA arbitrary-and-capricious; Loper Bright "best reading" of the statute the regulation purports to implement); (ii) challenges to the agency's interpretation of the regulation (Auer / Kisor); (iii) challenges to the agency's reliance on FAM constructions (Skidmore).

### 7.5 5 U.S.C. § 706 — standard of review on federal-court appeals

The Administrative Procedure Act at 5 U.S.C. § 706 governs federal-court review of agency action:

> "To the extent necessary to decision and when presented, the reviewing court shall decide all relevant questions of law, interpret constitutional and statutory provisions, and determine the meaning or applicability of the terms of an agency action. The reviewing court shall — (1) compel agency action unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed; and (2) hold unlawful and set aside agency action, findings, and conclusions found to be — (A) arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law; (B) contrary to constitutional right, power, privilege, or immunity; (C) in excess of statutory jurisdiction, authority, or limitations, or short of statutory right; (D) without observance of procedure required by law …"

(5 U.S.C. § 706.)

Three review grounds operate in E-2 federal challenges:

1. *§ 706(2)(A) — arbitrary and capricious.* The classic APA challenge. An agency denial that ignores material evidence, fails to articulate a rational connection between facts and decision, or relies on factors Congress did not intend can be set aside.
2. *§ 706(2)(C) — in excess of statutory authority.* Where the agency interprets the statute in a manner exceeding the statute's grant, the action is set aside. Post-Loper Bright, this ground is doctrinally more potent: courts are no longer required to defer to the agency's reasonable interpretation; they must independently determine whether the agency exceeded statutory authority.
3. *§ 706(1) — unreasonable delay; mandamus.* Where the agency has unreasonably delayed action on a petition, the court may compel adjudication. This ground operates in E-2 most often where I-129 petitions sit pending without explanation for extended periods.

Federal-court review of E-2 denials is operationally rare — the consular-post denial is not directly reviewable (the doctrine of consular nonreviewability, *Department of State v. Muñoz*, 144 S. Ct. 1812 (2024), confirms the doctrine post-2024); the USCIS denial is reviewable through the AAO and then federal court. The viable federal challenges are typically (i) USCIS denials of I-129 E-2 petitions where the AAO has affirmed, and (ii) mandamus actions on unreasonable adjudication delay. The Loper Bright framework operates in the first category; mandamus is independent.

### 7.6 Practical consequence for our practice

Pre-2024 boilerplate that cited Chevron should be retired. RFE responses and APA challenges should now anchor in (i) the statutory text directly, (ii) the regulation as authoritative absent APA vulnerability, (iii) the FAM as Skidmore-persuasive, and (iv) the agency's interpretation of its own regulation as Auer / Kisor-deferred but Kisor-narrowed. *Loper Bright* did not invalidate any specific E-2 regulation; it altered the litigation posture for challenging interpretive overreach. The regulatory architecture remains intact; the deference floor moved.

The cover-letter drafting consequence: every cover letter that touches a discretionary substantiality, marginality, or essential-skills argument should now include a citation hierarchy that runs statute → regulation → FAM, with explicit acknowledgment that the FAM construction operates as Skidmore-persuasive guidance. The drafting move signals to the adjudicator that the firm is current on the deference posture and is not relying on a now-overruled Chevron framework.

### 7.7 Skidmore factors — detailed application to E-2 FAM constructions

The Skidmore framework asks whether the agency's interpretation has "power to persuade." The Court in *Skidmore* identified four factors: (i) thoroughness evident in the agency's consideration; (ii) validity of the agency's reasoning; (iii) consistency with earlier and later pronouncements; (iv) all those factors which give it power to persuade. Applied to the principal E-2 FAM constructions, the Skidmore weight varies.

*Inverted-sliding-scale construction of substantiality.* The construction has been agency practice since the 1980s, formalized in the 1997 regulatory revision, and consistently applied across subsequent revisions. Skidmore weight is high: the construction is thorough (it engages with the underlying delegation, the proportionality logic, and the operational reality of investments at varying scales); the reasoning is valid (the proportionality logic is internally coherent); the consistency is high (over four decades of uniform application); and the persuasive force is substantial (the construction is widely understood within the practitioner bar and within the industry). A federal-court challenge to the construction on Skidmore grounds would face high resistance.

*Five-year horizon for marginality future capacity.* The construction has been agency practice since the 1980s, articulated in *Matter of Ho*, 19 I&N Dec. 582 (BIA 1988), and consistently applied. Skidmore weight is moderate to high. The thoroughness is moderate (the agency has not articulated why five years rather than another period); the reasoning is moderate (a five-year horizon is reasonable but not uniquely so); the consistency is high (over three decades of uniform application); the persuasive force is moderate. A federal-court challenge could plausibly argue for a different horizon (e.g., three years for a fast-maturing service business, ten years for a heavy-capex industrial business). The challenge would not be frivolous post-Loper Bright; it might succeed if framed against the right factual record.

*Multi-factor test for executive/supervisory (seven factors at 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(a)).* The test has been agency practice since the 1990s and is consistently applied. Skidmore weight is high. The thoroughness is high (the seven factors are articulated with operational specificity); the reasoning is valid (the factors collectively measure the qualitative character of executive function); the consistency is high; the persuasive force is high.

*Multi-factor test for essential skills (nine factors at 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(b)).* The test has similar provenance and Skidmore weight. The "foreign-language-and-culture-alone" rule is doctrinally controversial in a narrow band of cases (where the language and culture knowledge is tightly paired with substantive knowledge, the rule operates harshly). A federal-court challenge to the rule could plausibly argue that the rule is over-inclusive; the challenge would not be frivolous.

The Skidmore analysis informs the firm's litigation posture: where a client matter presents facts that are well-aligned with the FAM construction, the firm should rely on the construction (its Skidmore weight is sufficient to defeat most challenges). Where a client matter presents facts that put pressure on the FAM construction, the firm should be prepared to argue against the construction on Skidmore grounds — the construction is persuasive but not binding, and the firm's argument can prevail if it engages the Skidmore factors directly.

### 7.8 Auer / Kisor — detailed application to E-2 regulatory interpretations

The Auer / Kisor framework operates on agency interpretations of agency regulations. *Kisor v. Wilkie*, 588 U.S. 558 (2019), articulated a five-step framework:

1. The regulation must be genuinely ambiguous (after applying ordinary tools of construction).
2. The agency's interpretation must be reasonable.
3. The interpretation must be the agency's authoritative or official position.
4. The interpretation must implicate the agency's substantive expertise.
5. The interpretation must be a fair and considered judgment, not a post-hoc rationalization.

Applied to E-2 regulatory provisions:

*"Real and active commercial or entrepreneurial undertaking" at 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(13).* The provision is genuinely ambiguous (what is "real and active"?); the agency's interpretation (requiring active operational steps toward production) is reasonable; the interpretation is authoritative (FAM and Policy Manual); the interpretation implicates substantive expertise (immigration adjudication); the interpretation is a considered judgment. Auer / Kisor deference applies and is high.

*"Subject to partial or total loss if investment fortunes reverse" at 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(12).* The provision is genuinely ambiguous (what level of loss-exposure suffices?); the agency's interpretation (excluding loans secured by enterprise assets) is reasonable; the interpretation is authoritative; etc. Auer / Kisor deference applies.

*"Ultimate control and responsibility for the enterprise's overall operation, or a major component thereof" at 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(17).* The provision is genuinely ambiguous (what counts as "ultimate control"?); the agency's interpretation (the seven-factor FAM test) is reasonable; etc. Auer / Kisor deference applies.

The doctrinal payoff: a federal-court challenge that targets the agency's *interpretation of regulatory text* (rather than the regulatory text itself or the underlying statute) faces the Auer / Kisor framework. The framework is more deferential to the agency than the post-Loper Bright statutory-interpretation framework. A litigant who has lost on Loper Bright grounds may still prevail on Auer / Kisor grounds, and vice versa. The firm's litigation posture should distinguish carefully between statutory-interpretation challenges (Loper Bright) and regulatory-interpretation challenges (Auer / Kisor).

### 7.9 Charming Betsy — detailed application to E-2 treaty-construction questions

The *Charming Betsy* canon operates on treaty-construction questions. Applied to E-2:

*Nationality determination.* Where the treaty defines nationality (e.g., a BIT's nationality clause), the treaty definition controls the regulatory construction. An agency interpretation that narrows or expands the treaty definition faces *Charming Betsy* resistance.

*Enterprise nationality (50% rule).* The 50% rule is a regulatory construction (8 CFR § 214.2(e)(3)) that applies to all treaty-country enterprises. Where a specific BIT's enterprise-nationality clause is broader (e.g., it includes enterprises incorporated in the treaty country regardless of human shareholding), the BIT's clause arguably controls under *Charming Betsy*. The agency's uniform application of the 50% rule to all treaty countries — without regard to specific BIT provisions — is, in the most aggressive litigation posture, vulnerable to *Charming Betsy* challenge in narrow cases.

*Reciprocity adjustments.* The Department of State's reciprocity-schedule decisions are made under the treaty framework and are entitled to substantial respect. *Charming Betsy* operates here only at the margins.

The *Charming Betsy* framework is operationally rare in E-2 adjudication — most cases do not turn on treaty-text construction. But in the cases where the treaty text is dispositive (or where the agency's interpretation is at variance with the treaty text), *Charming Betsy* can be the decisive doctrinal lever.

### 7.10 Layered deference architecture — synthesis

The post-Loper Bright deference architecture for E-2 consequently operates on multiple layers:

| Source of authority | Deference framework | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| INA § 101(a)(15)(E)(ii) — statutory text | *De novo* judicial construction (Loper Bright) | Court's independent judgment |
| 8 CFR § 214.2(e) — regulatory text | APA review (5 U.S.C. § 706) | Authoritative absent successful APA challenge |
| 22 CFR § 41.51 — regulatory text | APA review | Authoritative absent successful APA challenge |
| Agency interpretation of 8 CFR § 214.2(e) regulatory language | Auer / Kisor | Deferential, subject to Kisor narrowing |
| Agency interpretation of 22 CFR § 41.51 regulatory language | Auer / Kisor | Deferential, subject to Kisor narrowing |
| 9 FAM 402.9 — agency guidance | Skidmore | Persuasive, weight per Skidmore factors |
| USCIS Policy Manual Vol. 2, Pt. F — agency guidance | Skidmore | Persuasive |
| AAO non-precedent decisions | Persuasive only | Within USCIS, internal precedent |
| BIA precedential decisions | Binding within agency adjudication | Subject to APA review |
| Underlying treaty text | *Charming Betsy*; treaty self-execution | Constraint on agency interpretation |

The synthesis is doctrinally complex but operationally tractable. A practitioner mounting a federal challenge to an E-2 denial should map the challenge across the layers: which arguments target the statute (Loper Bright), which target the regulation (APA), which target the agency's interpretation of the regulation (Auer / Kisor), which target the FAM construction (Skidmore), and which target the treaty-construction question (*Charming Betsy*). Each layer has its own framework; each framework has its own preponderance of doctrinal force; each layer's argument must be developed independently.

### 8.1 NDAA 2023 § 5502 — three-year residency for citizens-by-investment

Public Law 117-263 § 5502 (Dec. 23, 2022) tightened E-2 eligibility for nationals of countries that grant citizenship by investment. The provision requires three years of residency in the treaty country before E-2 eligibility attaches.

**Statutory text (verbatim).**

> "An alien shall not be considered a national of a foreign state for purposes of section 101(a)(15)(E) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (8 U.S.C. 1101(a)(15)(E)) unless the alien has been domiciled in the foreign state for a continuous period of not less than 3 years at any point before applying for a nonimmigrant visa under such section."

(NDAA 2023 § 5502, 136 Stat. 3253.)

**Implementation.** The Department of State implemented the requirement through Cable 22 STATE 130543 (Dec. 2022) and incorporated the requirement into 9 FAM 402.9-4(A) and -4(E) through 2023 updates. USCIS implemented the requirement through internal operational guidance issued in 2023.

**Affected countries.** The provision principally affects countries with active citizenship-by-investment programs. Illustrative (currency-flagged):

- Grenada (Citizenship by Investment Programme).
- Türkiye (for naturalized-by-investment, not by birth).
- Bulgaria (until 2022, when the program was suspended; legacy applicants affected).
- Various Caribbean nations (Antigua and Barbuda, Dominica, St. Kitts and Nevis, St. Lucia) — though only those with active E-2 treaty status.

The provision is the most significant 2020s amendment to E-2 doctrine.

**Doctrinal questions.** Several open questions remain:

1. *Domicile vs. residency.* The statute uses "domiciled," which has a more demanding meaning than "resided." The Department of State has interpreted domicile to mean "principal place of residence with the intent to maintain it as such" — closer to the immigration-law domicile standard than the tax-law domicile standard.
2. *"At any point before applying"* — the provision permits the three-year residency to be at any prior point, not necessarily contiguous with the application. An applicant who lived in the treaty country for three years, departed, and returns ten years later would (under the literal text) qualify. The agencies have been flexible on this point.
3. *Pre-effective-date applications.* The provision applies to applications filed on or after enactment. Pre-enactment-filed applications retain pre-NDAA eligibility.
4. *Dual nationals.* A dual national who acquired one nationality by birth and another by naturalization has a choice; the by-birth nationality is not subject to NDAA § 5502. The by-investment nationality is.

*Flag for Supervisor: confirm currency of three-year residency rule as applied 2024–2026 and verify implementation in current FAM text.*

### 8.2 USCIS Policy Alert PA-2022-04 — E spouse work authorization

USCIS Policy Alert PA-2022-04 (Mar. 18, 2022) and the operationalizing 2022 USCIS Policy Manual update established that E-2 spouses are work-authorized incident to status, eliminating the I-765 EAD requirement. As of 2026, this is settled policy.

**Mechanics.** The I-94 issued on E-2 admission for the spouse bears the "E-2S" annotation that serves as evidence of work authorization. The annotation operates independently of any I-765 filing; the E-2S annotation is sufficient for I-9 employment-eligibility verification. (8 CFR § 274a.12(a)(17) lists E-2 spouses among the categories authorized for employment incident to status.)

**Children.** Note that the policy alert addressed only spouses, not unmarried children under 21. Children of E-2 principals are derivative E-2 status holders but are not work-authorized incident to status; they may seek separate work authorization through other routes (typically not E-based).

*Flag for Supervisor: confirm the I-94 annotation convention has not changed since 2024; some 2024 entry-stamp variations have been observed at certain ports of entry.*

### 8.4 2024 USCIS fee final rule — 89 FR 6196

The USCIS fee final rule, published at 89 FR 6196 (Jan. 31, 2024), became effective April 1, 2024. The rule substantially restructured I-129 fees, including the I-129 fee for E classification.

**Pre-2024 fee.** I-129 E-2 filing fee was USD 460.00.

**Post-2024 fee.** The 2024 final rule established tiered I-129 fees based on petitioner size and filing type. For E-2 filings, the standard I-129 fee is USD 1,015.00, with a USD 460.00 reduced fee for small employers (defined as 25 or fewer full-time equivalent employees) and a USD 530.00 reduced fee for nonprofit employers. The Asylum Program Fee adds USD 600.00 for standard employers, USD 300.00 for small employers, and zero for nonprofits.

**Premium processing.** Premium processing fee for I-129 E-2 was increased to USD 2,805.00 (from prior USD 2,500.00) by the 2024 final rule.

**MRV fee.** The MRV fee at consular posts (governed by 22 CFR § 22.1, not by the USCIS final rule) remains USD 315.00 per applicant.

The fee changes are operationally significant for cost projections in client engagements but do not affect the substantive E-2 elements.

### 8.8 EOIR — not directly operative

EOIR has no direct adjudicatory role in initial E-2 visa or status grants. EOIR enters the picture only on removal proceedings where a denied or expired E-2 holder is placed before an immigration judge. No EOIR practice manual update in the trailing 24 months materially alters E-2 doctrine.

The post-2024 EOIR posture on bona-fide-nonimmigrant-intent challenges in removal proceedings remains stable: an E-2 holder who has overstayed or violated status faces removal under INA § 237(a)(1)(B) or (C) and may seek voluntary departure or other relief under standard removal-proceeding frameworks.

## 9. Sub-type regulatory anchors

The four E-2 sub-types are doctrinally distinct configurations of the same statutory and regulatory stack. Each anchors to specific FAM and 8 CFR provisions. The treatment below addresses each sub-type's structural elements and includes worked anonymized examples calibrated to the firm's standard exemplar conventions.

### 9.1 Subtype 1 — Individual treaty investor

**Anchor.** 9 FAM 402.9-7(1) + 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(2) + 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(16).

**Doctrinal frame.** A single human Beneficiary, treaty-country national, owns ≥ 50% of the Petitioner directly or holds operational control via managerial governance, and personally develops and directs the enterprise. The investment chain is personal: lawful funds (sale proceeds, savings, gift, inheritance, non-collateralized loan) traced from origin to US deployment.

**Structural elements.** Six structural elements must be established:

1. *Treaty-country nationality of the Beneficiary.* Established by passport, certificate of nationality, and (for naturalized-by-investment cases) three-year residency proof per NDAA § 5502.
2. *50% ownership or controlling interest.* Established by share register, articles of organization, operating agreement, board resolution. For controlling interest below 50%, the controlling agreement (voting trust, special-share class, management agreement) must be documented.
3. *Real and operating enterprise.* Established by state registration, business license, employer-identification number, tax filings, lease, utility records, employment records, customer/supplier records.
4. *Substantial investment.* Established by source-of-funds documentation (bank statements, sale proceeds documents, gift letter with donor SOF, etc.) and deployment documentation (transfer records to US accounts, deployment to enterprise — equipment purchase, lease deposits, payroll, marketing, etc.). The proportionality argument (capital invested ÷ total cost of enterprise) is made in the cover letter.
5. *Non-marginality.* Established by five-year financial projection that crosses the minimal-living threshold for the Beneficiary's family, plus a margin. Where the projection does not show excess income, the significant-economic-contribution escape (9 FAM 402.9-6(E)) is engaged via job creation, technology development, or US-industry contribution.
6. *Develop and direct.* Established by Beneficiary's role description, organizational chart, management agreement, business plan with Beneficiary's strategic role articulated.

**Mandatory provisions.** 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(2) (definition); (e)(12) (at-risk); (e)(13) (real and operating); (e)(14) (substantiality); (e)(15) (marginality); (e)(16) (develop and direct). 9 FAM 402.9-4(A) (nationality), 402.9-4(B) (≥ 50%), 402.9-6(A)–(E), 402.9-7(1).

**Discretion locus.** Substantiality (no fixed floor) and marginality (future-capacity construction) are the two adjudicator-facing soft elements. The cover-letter argument must close both via documented industry comparison and a five-year financial projection that crosses minimal-living plus a margin.

**Worked exemplar — Subtype 1 exemplar (US LLC, Q1 2026 filing).** Pattern: Beneficiary is a Turkish national who naturalized in 2010, with continuous Türkiye residency from 1982 to 2018 (NDAA § 5502 readily satisfied). Beneficiary owns 100% of a Delaware LLC. Investment: USD 285,000 deployed to lease a commercial space, purchase equipment for a specialty food production operation, and fund six months of payroll for three full-time employees. Source of funds: USD 195,000 from sale of a Türkiye residential property (2024); USD 90,000 from family savings (10-year bank statement chain). Five-year projection shows growth to USD 750,000 annual revenue with eight full-time employees by year five — non-marginal on income; significant economic contribution argued in the alternative. Subtype 1 anchor: 9 FAM 402.9-7(1) develop-and-direct (100% owner, sole manager); 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(2) (treaty investor definition); 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(14) (substantiality — USD 285,000 against total enterprise cost of approximately USD 320,000 for a small specialty-food operation, > 89% of total cost); 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(15) (marginality — five-year projection plus job-creation contribution).

The exemplar illustrates the modal Subtype 1 pattern: small US LLC, six-figure investment, sole or co-owner Beneficiary, individual SOF chain, five-year financial projection crossing the minimal-living threshold.

### 9.2 Subtype 2 — Corporate-owned investor

**Anchor.** 9 FAM 402.9-4(B) + 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(3).

**Doctrinal frame.** A treaty-country corporation invests in a US subsidiary; the human Beneficiary qualifies through the corporate ownership chain. Two ownership layers must clear the ≥ 50% treaty test: the foreign parent's ownership by treaty-country nationals, and the US subsidiary's ownership by the foreign parent. Both layers must trace.

**Ownership-chain doctrine.** The chain operates as follows:

1. The US subsidiary (Petitioner) must be ≥ 50% owned by the foreign parent (or by treaty-country individuals or by other treaty-country entities that themselves clear the test).
2. The foreign parent must be ≥ 50% owned (directly or through additional layers) by treaty-country natural persons.
3. Each layer must trace to documentary proof: share registers at each level, board resolutions on capital flows, capital tables.

**Treaty-national arithmetic.** The 50% test operates multiplicatively in some layered structures. Department of State practice has accepted the multiplicative-aggregation approach in some cases (where the chain product is ≥ 50%), but the most defensive practice is to ensure each layer independently clears 50%. Worked example: foreign parent owned 70% by treaty-country individuals and 30% by non-treaty-country individuals; foreign parent owns 80% of US subsidiary. Multiplicative aggregation: 70% × 80% = 56% — clears. Layer-by-layer: foreign parent layer clears 50% (70%); US subsidiary layer clears 50% (foreign parent's 80%) — clears.

The doctrinal warning: a layered structure with one layer below 50% (e.g., foreign parent 60%-owned by treaty nationals; intermediate holding company 40%-owned by foreign parent and 60%-owned by non-treaty individuals; US subsidiary 100%-owned by intermediate) fails because the intermediate layer does not clear.

**Mandatory provisions.** 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(3) (corporate ownership); 9 FAM 402.9-4(B) (50% rule applied to enterprise nationality); 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(13) (real and operating); 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(14) (substantiality at the US-subsidiary level); 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(15) (marginality at the US-subsidiary level); 9 FAM 402.9-6(F) (subsidiary considerations).

**Discretion locus.** The ownership trace is technically demanding but not discretionary; it is satisfied or not on the documents (share registers, board resolutions, capital tables). Discretion concentrates at substantiality (parent-to-subsidiary capitalization) and at the US subsidiary's bona fide-enterprise proof.

**Worked exemplar — Subtype 2 exemplar (foreign parent → US subsidiary, manufacturing sector, Q3 2025 filing).** Pattern: foreign parent is a German GmbH (50.1% owned by two German natural-person founders, 49.9% owned by a German private equity firm in turn 100%-owned by German individuals). German parent invested USD 4.2 million in a Delaware corporation US subsidiary (100% owned by German parent). US subsidiary operates a precision manufacturing facility in North Carolina employing 22 full-time workers. Beneficiary is a German national, the German parent's Chief Operating Officer, deployed to the US subsidiary as President. The Subtype 2 anchor governs the *enterprise's* treaty-country status; the Beneficiary's role as US subsidiary President is anchored separately (as a Subtype 3 case, executive). Note: this is the typical Subtype 2 + Subtype 3 dual-anchor pattern.

### 9.3 Subtype 3 — Executive / supervisory employee

**Anchor.** 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(a) + 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(17).

**Doctrinal frame.** The Petitioner enterprise must already qualify (under Subtype 1 or Subtype 2 logic). The Beneficiary is an executive (C-suite, VP, Director, Country Manager) with "great authority to determine the policy and direction" of the enterprise (9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(a)). The role test is qualitative, not titular; an officer in title who performs routine staff work fails. The regulatory trap is § 214.2(e)(17)'s "ultimate control and responsibility" language — adjudicators read this as requiring substantive authority, not nominal.

**The "executive" criterion.** "Executive" in the FAM and regulation imports the management-of-management concept: the Beneficiary directs other managers, sets strategic direction, controls enterprise resources. The criterion is borrowed by analogy from L-1A "executive capacity" (8 CFR § 214.2(l)(1)(ii)(C)) but is not identical; the L-1A executive standard is more specific (four-part test in the L-1A regulation), while the E-2 executive standard is broader and more qualitative.

**The "supervisory" criterion.** "Supervisory" is the alternative-track. The Beneficiary supervises subordinates, exercises judgment on personnel matters, conducts performance reviews. The supervisory standard is less demanding than the executive standard; a senior manager who supervises a department may qualify as supervisory even if she does not qualify as executive.

**The "primary function" analysis.** The duties must be "principally and primarily" executive or supervisory. The "anti-routine paragraph" — explicitly disclaiming substantive staff work as the Beneficiary's primary duty — is a doctrinal best practice anchored in the FAM's express exclusion of routine work. The cover letter should:

1. Affirmatively articulate the executive or supervisory duties and their proportion of the Beneficiary's working hours.
2. Negatively disclaim the routine duties and their proportion.
3. Provide an organizational chart placing the Beneficiary at the apex or near-apex.
4. Provide a list of direct reports with their titles and functions.
5. Provide examples of policy or strategic decisions the Beneficiary will make.

**Mandatory provisions.** 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(17). 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(a) (multi-factor: title, organizational placement, span of control, supervised employees' skill, salary, prior experience).

**Discretion locus.** All seven multi-factor elements at 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(a) are assessed on the record. The cover-letter draft must address each factor head-on; absence of a factor (e.g., span-of-control evidence) is a documented hole, not a permissible omission. The "anti-routine paragraph" — explicitly disclaiming substantive staff work as the Beneficiary's primary duty — is a doctrinal best practice anchored in the FAM's express exclusion of routine work.

**Worked exemplar — Subtype 3 exemplar (US subsidiary President, manufacturing sector, Q3 2025 filing).** Pattern: see Subtype 2 worked exemplar above for the enterprise foundation. Beneficiary is the German parent's Chief Operating Officer, deployed to the US subsidiary as President. Beneficiary's role: direct and policy-set the US subsidiary's operations; supervise the four-person executive team (CFO, COO, VP Sales, VP Operations); approve all expenditures > USD 100,000; hire, fire, and conduct performance reviews of executive team; report quarterly to German parent board. The cover letter affirms 90%+ of working hours on executive duties, with the residual 10% on cross-functional management. Subtype 3 anchor: 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(a) (executive capacity); 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(17) (ultimate control and responsibility for major component of enterprise — the US subsidiary). Salary: USD 285,000 base plus bonus. Span of control: 4 direct reports, 22 total US subsidiary employees. Title: President. Organizational placement: apex of US subsidiary, reporting to German parent COO. Prior experience: 12 years in management positions at German parent. Each FAM factor is addressed.

### 9.4 Subtype 4 — Essential-skills employee

**Anchor.** 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(b) + 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(18).

**Doctrinal frame.** Same enterprise-qualifying foundation as Subtype 3. The Beneficiary's specialized knowledge or skill must be essential to the efficient operation of the enterprise. The proof is the FAM's nine-factor test.

**The "essential vs. valuable" distinction.** A skill is "essential" when its absence would materially impair enterprise operation; a skill is merely "valuable" when its presence is helpful but not necessary. The distinction is doctrinally important. Adjudicators frequently confuse the two; the firm's RFE-response practice should sharply distinguish them. Essential skills are characterized by:

1. *Operational necessity.* The enterprise cannot operate efficiently without the skill.
2. *Specialized application.* The skill is applied to enterprise-specific processes (proprietary technology, unique production methods, specialized client relationships, particular regulatory frameworks).
3. *Scarcity.* Few others possess the skill; the skill is not readily available in the US labor market.

Valuable skills, by contrast, may be widely available, may be substitutable, may serve general (not enterprise-specific) functions.

**The "specialized knowledge" analysis.** Specialized knowledge — the close analogue of L-1B specialized knowledge but applied to E-2 — is the doctrinal framework for what makes a skill essential. Specialized knowledge is:

1. *Knowledge of enterprise-specific processes, products, services, research, equipment, techniques, management, or other interests of the employer.* (Borrowing from the L-1B definition at 8 CFR § 214.2(l)(1)(ii)(D), applied by analogy.)
2. *Advanced level of expertise within the enterprise.* The Beneficiary's expertise should be at a level that would not be readily replicated through hiring in the US labor market.

**The "not readily available in the US labor market" test.** This is the labor-market test. Adjudicators expect labor-market evidence — BLS data, industry surveys, recruiting records, posted job listings, salary surveys. The evidence should show:

1. The number of US workers with the relevant skill is low.
2. The salary required to recruit a US worker with the relevant skill is high (suggesting scarcity).
3. The Beneficiary's specific skill profile is even more rare than the general skill category.

The labor-market test is the most adjudicator-facing soft element of Subtype 4.

**Mandatory provisions.** 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(18). 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(b) (multi-factor).

**Discretion locus.** Two pressure points. First, "not readily available in US labor market" (§ 214.2(e)(18)) — adjudicators expect labor-market evidence. Second, the *temporal* dimension at FAM -7(2)(b): start-up skills may not remain essential after operations stabilize. The drafter must either argue durable essentiality (product improvement, quality control, unique-service activities) or honestly request limited-duration status. Misalignment between the temporal frame and the requested period of stay is a NOID predictor.

**Worked exemplar — Subtype 4 exemplar (US subsidiary technical specialist, energy sector, Q1 2024 filing).** Pattern: foreign parent in a specialized industrial sector (high-temperature ceramic substrate manufacturing for industrial energy applications), US subsidiary, Beneficiary holds technical product/market knowledge not readily available in US labor market. Beneficiary's role: lead the US subsidiary's high-temperature substrate calibration and quality-assurance protocols; train US-hired technical staff on proprietary calibration methodologies developed at the foreign parent over 15 years. Salary differential vs US peer market: 35% premium over BLS median for industrial-ceramics specialists, reflecting the specialized firm-specific knowledge. Labor-market evidence: BLS occupational data showing 47 industrial-ceramics specialists nationwide with the relevant high-temperature subspecialty; recruiting records from 8 US firms in the sector showing a 14-month average time-to-hire for the specialty; salary survey showing the firm-specific knowledge premium. Temporal durability: the proprietary calibration methodologies require 36+ months of in-system training; the Beneficiary's role will remain essential through year three of US operations, after which a transition plan to trained-up US staff is contemplated. Subtype 4 anchor: 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(b) (essential skills); 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(18). The cover letter addresses each of the nine FAM factors and includes the labor-market evidence and the temporal-durability argument.

### 9.5 Sub-type interaction matrix

A single matter may present sub-type ambiguity. The doctrinal calibrators:

| Signal | Subtype |
|---|---|
| Single human ≥ 50% owner of US LLC, personal SOF chain | 1 |
| Treaty-country corporate parent → US subsidiary; human is the controlling shareholder of the parent | 2 |
| Treaty-country parent → US subsidiary; human is an officer of the US subsidiary, not a controlling shareholder | 3 or 4 |
| Officer holds P&L authority and supervises substantial staff | 3 |
| Officer holds technical / product / market specialization, salary differential vs US peer market | 4 |
| Misclassification risk: BUS folder label or "investor" intake but actual fact pattern is employee-of-treaty-enterprise | Re-classify to 3 or 4 |

Misclassification defeats cases. The regulatory anchors above govern; intake labels do not. (See Demir's Part 4 for the misclassification-risk catalog.)

### 9.6 Dual-anchor patterns

Several common patterns combine sub-types:

1. *Subtype 2 + Subtype 3.* Treaty-country corporate parent owns US subsidiary; the human Beneficiary is an executive of the US subsidiary deployed by the parent. This is the most common foreign-direct-investment pattern; the enterprise qualifies under Subtype 2 logic, and the Beneficiary qualifies under Subtype 3 logic.
2. *Subtype 2 + Subtype 4.* Treaty-country corporate parent owns US subsidiary; the human Beneficiary is a technical specialist deployed by the parent. The enterprise qualifies under Subtype 2 logic, and the Beneficiary qualifies under Subtype 4 logic.
3. *Subtype 1 + Subtype 3 (rare).* Individual treaty-country investor owns US enterprise; she also brings a treaty-country-national executive employee. The investor qualifies under Subtype 1 logic; the executive qualifies under Subtype 3 logic.

The dual-anchor patterns require dual-track cover-letter drafting: the enterprise-qualifying argument runs on one anchor (Subtype 1 or Subtype 2); the Beneficiary-qualifying argument runs on the other anchor (Subtype 1, Subtype 3, or Subtype 4). Misalignment between the two tracks is a frequent RFE predictor.

## 10. Closing doctrinal observations

Three observations close this Part.

First, the E-2 regulatory architecture is statutorily thin and regulatorily thick. The INA hands the agencies a brief mandate ("substantial," "develop and direct"); the agencies have constructed a multi-decade gloss in 8 CFR § 214.2(e), 22 CFR § 41.51, and 9 FAM 402.9. *Loper Bright* did not destabilize that gloss but moved the deference floor. Practitioners should now cite to statute, regulation, and FAM in that hierarchy — and stop reflexively citing Chevron. The Auer / Kisor framework remains alive for agency interpretations of agency regulations; the Skidmore framework remains alive for FAM constructions; the *Charming Betsy* canon remains alive for treaty-consistent constructions. The deference architecture is more layered than the popular post-*Loper Bright* discourse suggests.

Second, the four sub-types are doctrinally distinct configurations, not procedural variants. Choosing the wrong sub-type produces a record that fails to clear the elements of the correct sub-type — even if the underlying facts are strong. The regulatory anchors (§ 9 above) are the calibration tool. Dual-anchor patterns (§ 9.6) are the most common configurations in practice; misalignment between the enterprise-qualifying argument and the Beneficiary-qualifying argument is a frequent failure mode.

Third, the trailing 24 months show no major regulatory upheaval, but the post-2024 deference shift, the 2022 NDAA residency rule, the 2022 spouse-EAD policy, the 2024 USCIS fee final rule, and the 2025 reciprocity adjustments together remake the practice quietly. Each must be carried in every cover letter that touches its facts. The practitioner who carries the 2018 PM-602-0145 substantiality framework and the pre-2024 Chevron citation alone is two policy generations behind.
