## 1. Intake checklist — the 35-question protocol

Intake is the highest-leverage hour of the case. A clean intake saves
40–60 hours of drafting and prevents four late-stage disasters:
sub-type misclassification (Imm-464-2023 pattern), SOF gap surfacing at
week 8, treaty eligibility surprise, dependent status discovered after
the principal files.

**Format.** Single 60-minute call (75 for Subtypes 3/4). Two attorneys
(lead + scribe), recorded with consent.

### 1.1 Identity + treaty country (Q1–Q6)

1. Full legal name as it appears on the passport (native script + ASCII transliteration).
2. Date of birth (ISO 8601).
3. Country of birth.
4. Country(ies) of current nationality. *If two: which passport will be used; is the non-treaty nationality renounceable or operationally invisible?*
5. Passport number, issuing authority, issue date, expiration date. *If expiring within 12 months, escalate.*
6. Treaty-country status confirmed against the State Department treaty list as of today (`travel.state.gov/.../treaty.html`). *Date the confirmation; print and tab the page.*

### 1.2 Prior US history (Q7–Q12)

7. Has the Beneficiary ever been admitted to the US? List every entry, status, dates (I-94 record pulled live).
8. Any prior visa refusals (any classification, any post)? *Section 214(b) refusals are routine; § 212(a)(6)(C) is escalation-grade.*
9. Any prior periods of unlawful presence, overstays, or out-of-status? *Calculate § 212(a)(9)(B) bars now, not at filing.*
10. Any removal, deportation, or voluntary departure proceedings?
11. Any criminal arrests, charges, or convictions — anywhere, ever (including expunged)? *Defense moves to Demir for edge-case framing.*
12. Any prior I-130, I-140, or AOS filings? *NIV intent posture changes if there is a pending immigrant petition.*

### 1.3 Business identity (Q13–Q18)

13. Petitioner legal name (verify exact match against Articles).
14. State of formation, formation date, FEIN.
15. Industry (NAICS code if known) and one-sentence business description.
16. Premises address. Owned, leased, or virtual? *Virtual offices flag escalation; real-and-operating risk.*
17. Current US employee count (W-2; not 1099 unless documented). *Below 2 W-2s on a renewal flags marginality risk.*
18. Most recent revenue (gross + net, last completed tax year). *Bare projections without trailing actuals are an RFE magnet on renewal.*

### 1.4 Ownership intent (Q19–Q23)

19. Sub-type self-identification: investor (1, 2) vs employee (3, 4)? *Detector caveat per `_E2-SUBTYPE-TAXONOMY.md` § 2 — folder labels lie; verify against ownership chain.*
20. If investor: percentage ownership of Petitioner, and identity + nationality of every other owner ≥ 5 %.
21. If employee: identity of the qualifying enterprise (foreign parent or US subsidiary), and proof the enterprise is currently treaty-qualified.
22. If borderline (Subtype 1 vs 4 — the Imm-464 hazard): collect both salary-differential evidence AND ownership documents until classification is locked.
23. Title and function the Beneficiary will hold post-approval.

### 1.5 Source of funds (Q24–Q29)

24. Total invested or to-be-invested amount (USD), with date of each tranche.
25. Origin of funds: salary, sale of property, inheritance, gift, loan (collateralized how?), corporate parent transfer.
26. Currency of origin and conversion path. *Note FX dates; high-volatility currencies need TÜFE/CPI-style inflation-adjustment paragraphs — see § 11.1.*
27. Bank account chain: which institution received funds at each step?
28. Any cash transactions ≥ USD 10,000? *Anything in cash beyond a single threshold = escalation; build the parallel paper trail before drafting.*
29. Any loan secured by the assets of the enterprise? *Per 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(12), this is impermissible "investment" — disqualify and rebuild.*

### 1.6 Dependents (Q30–Q32)

30. Spouse: name, DOB, nationality, current immigration status (in or out of US), prior US history.
31. Children under 21 unmarried: count, names, DOBs, nationalities, current statuses.
32. Marriage and birth records held? Translation status (8 CFR § 103.2(b)(3)).

### 1.7 Timeline urgency (Q33–Q35)

33. When does the Beneficiary need to be in E-2 status (target date)?
34. What is driving the date — school, lease, business launch, regulatory deadline?
35. Any travel planned in the next 120 days that interacts with the filing? *International travel mid-COS abandons the COS — see § 4.3.*

> **Whitfield, 90-second rule.** If at intake close I cannot articulate,
> in three sentences, *who the principal investor is*, *which sub-type
> applies*, and *where the SOF chain starts and ends*, the intake is
> incomplete. Re-schedule before estimating fees.

## 2. Document collection sequence — the 60-90 day build window

Order matters. Gating documents come first. Overnight-produce documents
come last so they do not stall the build sitting in queue.

### 2.1 Pacing model (60-day standard build)

| Day | Phase | Client-side ask | Entity-side ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | Intake + retainer | Passports (all family); prior US visas + I-94; CV | Articles, Operating Agreement, EIN letter, lease |
| 4–10 | Treaty + ownership lock | Co-owner passports/EADs; marriage/birth certs (with translations queued) | Member resolutions; cap table; foreign parent corporate docs (Subtypes 2/3/4) |
| 11–20 | SOF chain assembly | Origin-of-funds documents (Tapu, sale contracts, inheritance decree, gift letter); 24 months personal bank statements covering origin → wire | Wire confirmations (parent-to-subsidiary for Subtypes 2/3/4) |
| 21–35 | Substantiality + at-risk | — | Spending reconciliation table; vendor invoices; build-out receipts; equipment invoices |
| 36–50 | Real-and-operating + marginality | — | Commercial lease, premises photos, payroll register, Form 941, W-2/W-3 stubs, business bank statements, business plan |
| 51–58 | Cover letter draft + cross-review | Beneficiary's signed Notice of Intent to Depart; dependent NOIDs | Petitioner's signature on I-129 + I-129E |
| 59–60 | Filing prep | — | G-1650 ACH form, fee schedule confirmation |

### 2.2 90-day extended build (Subtypes 2/3/4)

Add 15 days front (foreign-document procurement, apostille, certified translation) and 15 days back (audit-quality reconciliation between parent's audited financials and I-129E investment amount — see § 11.2).

### 2.3 Ask order — first vs last

**Ask first** (gates downstream): passports (whole family); prior US visas + I-94s; Articles + Operating Agreement (and parent's Esas Sözleşme for Subtypes 2/3/4); origin-of-funds anchor (Tapu, sale contract, inheritance decree, audited balance sheet).

**Ask last** (overnight produce): Notice of Intent to Depart; Petitioner signature on I-129/I-129E; G-1650 ACH; client review of counsel-drafted personal statements.

> **Whitfield rule.** Never ask for 24 months of bank statements at week
> 6. You'll get them at week 9, colliding with cover-letter draft. Ask
> week 2; receive week 4; reconcile week 5 in parallel with substantiality.

## 3. Packet assembly — tab-by-tab choreography

The Preparation Manual lists Tabs A–L. Part 3 deepens the *assembly
choreography*: build order, defensive-paragraph placement rules, cover-letter cadence.

### 3.1 Build order vs filing order

Tab A through L is the *filing order* the adjudicator sees. The *build
order* is different — I build outward from the strongest evidence:

| Build sequence | Tab | Why first |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | E (SOF chain) | If this fails, the case fails. Built first so weakness surfaces early. |
| 2 | D (Ownership) | Locks classification. Locks I-129E ownership-percentage field. |
| 3 | C (Treaty nationality) | Smallest tab, fastest close, dependencies few. |
| 4 | F (Substantiality) | Computed from D + E. Cannot draft until those settle. |
| 5 | G (Real & operating + marginality) | Bulk of the file; build last on entity side because numbers move. |
| 6 | H (Develop & direct OR employee role) | Cross-references G's org chart and D's resolutions. |
| 7 | A (Forms) | Filled at the end; numbers must match cover letter exactly. |
| 8 | B (Cover letter) | Drafted after C–H exist, because the cover letter is the *index of what was proven*, not the *argument that hopes to be proven*. |
| 9 | I, J, K, L (Dependents) | Trail the principal; never block the principal. |

### 3.2 Cover-letter drafting cadence — three passes

1. **Skeleton (Day 50).** Section headings, `(Exhibit: TBD)` placeholders, element-by-element anchors. ~1,500 words. Goal: confirm the file proves what it says it proves.
2. **Substance (Day 53).** Fill prose; replace TBDs with verbatim index cross-references; insert defensive paragraphs (§ 3.4) and sub-type calibrations (§ 11). 3,500–5,000 words (Subtype 1); 4,000–6,500 (Subtypes 3/4).
3. **Adjudicator-ergonomics (Day 57).** Read aloud. Cut anything an officer skims past. Move strongest evidence to first paragraph of each section. Insert 90-second headers (§ 3.5).

> **Whitfield, margin-note style.** "I would cut this paragraph" — the
> phrase I use 30 times per review. The cover letter is a guided tour
> of the exhibits, not a brief. If a paragraph does not point at an
> exhibit, it is decoration.

### 3.3 Binder mechanics

- Tab dividers labelled to the index heading verbatim.
- Each Tab opens with its exhibit list (from `manuals/03-EXHIBIT-INDEX-TEMPLATE.md`).
- Within each Tab, exhibits in cover-letter citation order — never chronological. The adjudicator should never flip backward.
- Bates-stamp packet bottom-right; pin-cites for any document > 5 pages.
- Color rule: cover letter white; dividers cream; indexes white. No flourishes.

### 3.4 Defensive paragraph placement

Defensive paragraphs (Demir's catalog — see Part 4) live in the cover
letter, not in the exhibits. Placement rule:

| Defensive theme | Placement | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Tapu / foreign title-deed explanation | Section IV, first time the exhibit is cited | Subtypes 1/2 with Turkish real estate in SOF |
| TÜFE / inflation rent escalation | Section IV, footnote-style block quote | Subtypes 1/2 with rental income SOF |
| Bordro / foreign payroll convention | Section VI (marginality) | Any subtype using foreign payroll evidence |
| Esas Sözleşme / foreign Articles | Section III | Subtypes 2/3/4 |
| Yönetim Kurulu Kararı / board resolution | Section III or IV | Subtypes 2/3/4 |
| Anti-routine work paragraph | Section VII (executive) | Subtype 3 |
| Skills-not-readily-available paragraph | Section VII (essential skills) | Subtype 4 |
| Time-limited essentiality framing | Section VII | Subtype 4 if duration < permanent |
| Joint-treaty / non-treaty ownership | Section II | Any sub-type with mixed ownership |

### 3.5 Adjudicator-ergonomics: the 90-second header

The first 90 seconds decide whether the case is approved on first read
or queued for RFE. Page 1 of the cover letter answers, in five lines:
*who* (Beneficiary + nationality); *what* (sub-type + posture); *how
much* (investment + ownership %); *where* (industry + state); *why now*
(extension/COS/new + target date). Then a one-paragraph summary of what
each Tab proves. Page 1 is for the officer; pages 2–N are for the file.

## 4. Filing-route decision tree

### 4.1 The four routes

| Route | When | Forms | Adjudicator | Output |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Consular new** | Beneficiary abroad, no prior E-2 | DS-160 + DS-156E | Consular officer | Visa stamp |
| **Consular renewal** | Beneficiary abroad (or willing to depart and return), prior E-2 expired or expiring | Same as above | Consular officer | New visa stamp |
| **USCIS COS** | Beneficiary in US on another status | I-129 + I-129E | USCIS service center | Status only — no visa stamp |
| **USCIS Extension** | Beneficiary in US on prior E-2 | Same as COS | USCIS service center | Status extension only |

### 4.2 Decision tree (read top-down)

```
Is the Beneficiary inside the US right now?
├── NO → CONSULAR ROUTE (new or renewal)
│        Sub-decision: which post?
│           - Treaty country home post (Ankara, Frankfurt) — preferred
│           - Third-country post — only with consulate-shopping rationale
│             documented in writing (avoid frivolous shopping)
└── YES → Is the Beneficiary already in E-2 status?
         ├── YES → USCIS EXTENSION (I-129)
         └── NO  → Does the Beneficiary plan to travel abroad in next 90 days?
                  ├── YES → CONSULAR (don't COS — travel abandons it)
                  └── NO  → USCIS COS
                           Caveat: COS gives status only, not visa stamp.
                           Beneficiary CANNOT leave US until consular stamp obtained.
```

### 4.3 Visa stamping vs status only

USCIS COS and Extension give *status only*. To re-enter after travel,
the Beneficiary must visit a consulate abroad for the *stamp* — a
separate adjudication; the USCIS approval does not bind the consular
officer. For Beneficiaries who travel internationally on business
(disproportionately Subtypes 2/3/4), consular is often *strategically*
better even when COS is *legally* available — five-year multi-entry
stamp in one step, not two.

### 4.4 Timeline implications by route

| Route | Receipt to outcome | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Consular new | 60–120 days from DS-160 submit (interview wait dominates) | See § 5.2 for post-by-post wait times |
| Consular renewal | Same; interview waiver may apply at some posts |
| USCIS COS regular | 4–8 months (CSC/VSC); see § 5.1 |
| USCIS COS premium | 15 business days (see § 6) |
| USCIS Extension regular | 4–8 months; bridge under 8 CFR § 274a.12 if work authorized |
| USCIS Extension premium | 15 business days |

## 5. Timeline norms

### 5.1 USCIS service center windows (median, trailing 12 months)

| Center | Receipt → Biometrics (when applicable) | Receipt → Adjudication (regular) | Receipt → RFE (if issued) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CSC** (California) | n/a for I-129 | 5–7 months | 90–120 days |
| **VSC** (Vermont) | n/a for I-129 | 6–8 months | 100–140 days |
| **NSC** (Nebraska) | n/a for I-129 | 5–9 months | 90–130 days |
| **TSC** (Texas) | n/a for I-129 | 6–10 months | 110–150 days |

I-539 / I-539A dependents track at biometrics 30–60 days after I-129 receipt at all four centers.

### 5.2 Consular interview wait by post (median, as of 2026-04 trailing quarter)

| Post | Interview wait | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Ankara** | 30–60 days | E-2 visa unit handles most appointments |
| **Frankfurt** | 21–45 days | High-volume; well-trained adjudicators |
| **Mumbai** | 60–120 days | Volume pressure; expect rigor |
| **Mexico City** | 14–35 days | Fast lane for E-2 |
| **Tokyo** | 21–45 days | Calibrated; rare RFEs in-post |

Figures shift quarterly. Pull live numbers from `travel.state.gov`
before quoting the client. Planning estimates only — never commitments.

### 5.3 RFE response windows

USCIS RFEs typically allow **87 days**; NOIDs **30 days**. Calendar at
issuance + 7, + 14, + 21 for internal milestones. Never burn the full
window; file at issuance + 60 (RFE) or + 21 (NOID).

## 6. Premium processing decision framework

Premium under INA § 286(u) and 8 CFR § 103.7(e)(1) gives USCIS 15
business days to *act* — approve, deny, RFE, or NOID. Premium does NOT
increase approval rates; it compresses the calendar.

### 6.1 When to elect premium

| Situation | Elect premium? |
|---|---|
| Client has an immovable start date < 6 months out | Yes |
| Client's H-1B / L-1 / F-1 OPT expires < 4 months out and COS bridge needs to land | Yes |
| Subtype 2/3/4 with parent-driven board calendar (foreign parent's audit-cycle deadline) | Yes |
| Subtype 1 with thin marginality file and known weak adjudicator queue | **No** — buying a faster RFE is buying nothing |
| Renewal where adjudicator history at the center is benign | Optional; case-by-case |
| Client's funds have not yet fully transferred (still pending wires at filing) | **No** — file with regular processing while SOF closes |

### 6.2 The 15-day clock

Clock starts when the I-907 fee is accepted (not filed). RFEs re-trigger
a fresh 15-day window upon response receipt. NOIDs do *not* re-trigger;
the premium fee is consumed at NOID issuance.

> **Whitfield rule on I-907.** Do not file I-907 simultaneously with a
> packet you have not stress-tested. Premium turns a fixable 8-month-RFE
> into a fixable 30-day-NOID. Same officer doubts; less time to cure.

## 7. RFE response craft — pattern library

An RFE is the officer telling you, in writing, exactly what is between
you and approval — a gift, if read correctly. The most common drafting
failure is responding to the *category* of the RFE rather than the
*specific words* the officer used.

### 7.1 Universal RFE response architecture

Every RFE response follows the same skeleton: (1) verbatim restatement
of officer's concern — no paraphrasing; (2) one-page index mapping each
concern → new exhibit(s); (3) walkthrough of 1–3 paragraphs per concern;
(4) defensive context where cultural/jurisdictional framing helps; (5)
one-sentence re-cite of controlling authority — do not lecture; (6) new
exhibits, Bates-stamped continuing from the original packet.

### 7.2 Substantiality RFE — model response

**Officer's typical concern.** Investment "does not appear substantial in relation to the total cost of the enterprise."

**Diagnostic.** Either (a) I-129E investment-amount field disagreed with the cover letter, or (b) proportionality fell below the inverted sliding scale (9 FAM 402.9-6(B)).

**Response.** (1) Reconcile investment amount across I-129E, MITA, cover letter; acknowledge drift, provide audited figure. (2) Re-compute proportionality with current numbers — show the math. (3) Cite inverted sliding scale and place the case on it. (4) Fresh exhibit: updated balance sheet or business-plan total-cost figure, signed and dated post-RFE. (5) If officer's math is wrong, say so politely — quote the officer's figure and demonstrate the arithmetic error.

### 7.3 Marginality RFE — model response

**Officer's typical concern.** Enterprise "has not been demonstrated to produce more than minimal living for the Beneficiary and family within five years."

**Diagnostic.** Almost always insufficient W-2 evidence, or projections without trailing-actuals support.

**Response.** (1) Lead with W-2 evidence: payroll register, Form 941, individual W-2s for non-Beneficiary employees — most decisive; put it first. (2) Trailing-12-months P&L tying revenue to projections. (3) Updated 5-year projections with industry data (IBISWorld/Statista/federal labor-market). (4) Defensive paragraph citing *Matter of Walsh & Pollard*, 20 I&N Dec. 60 (BIA 1988) — minimal living is the test, not grandeur. (5) Borderline cases: state W-2 count, payroll total, dates, tabs.

> **Subtype 1 calibration.** Subtype 1 marginality RFEs win on W-2
> count, not projection elegance. Zero non-Beneficiary W-2s at RFE
> issuance = first firm action is to hire one (properly, prospectively)
> and document the hire in the response.

### 7.4 Ownership RFE — model response

**Officer's typical concern.** Treaty-country nationals "have not been shown to own at least 50 percent of the petitioner."

**Diagnostic.** Either (a) cap table fails ≥ 50 % treaty nationality, or (b) the chain (Articles → OA → MITA → current cap) has a gap.

**Response.** (1) Reconstruct ownership chain from formation, every transfer documented. (2) Fresh one-page cap table reconciliation with co-owner nationalities verified against passports/EADs. (3) Dual-national co-owner: cite 9 FAM 402.9-4(A); demonstrate treaty nationality is operative (passport used, residence). (4) Exactly 50 %: argue equal control; cite *Matter of Izumi*, 22 I&N Dec. 169 (BIA 1998). (5) Beneficial ≠ legal owner: defer to Part 4 (Demir).

### 7.5 Source-of-funds RFE — model response

**Officer's typical concern.** Record "does not adequately demonstrate the source and lawful possession of the invested funds."

**Diagnostic.** Gap in the SOF chain — origin unclear, conversion undocumented, wire-to-deployment unmatched, or cash gap > USD 10,000.

**Response.** (1) Print the SOF chain table (manuals/03 § E); annotate thin rows with fresh exhibits. (2) Defensive paragraph for any foreign jurisdictional element (Tapu, inheritance decree, parent corporate transfer). (3) Inflation-adjusted historical proceeds: append TÜFE/CPI escalation and translator's note. (4) Cash transactions > USD 10,000: pair with bank deposit slips and sworn statement. (5) Loans: produce the loan agreement, demonstrate non-collateralization by enterprise assets (8 CFR § 214.2(e)(12)), include lender's statement.

> **Subtype 1 exemplar (US LLC, Q1 2026 filing) demonstrated this
> pattern by** layering the Tapu defensive paragraph, TÜFE footnote,
> and co-owner-nationality reconciliation in a single 4-page response
> that resolved a 17-page RFE on first pass.

### 7.6 At-risk RFE — model response

**Officer's typical concern.** Funds not "irrevocably committed and at risk in the commercial sense."

**Diagnostic.** Funds sit in cash, a non-operating account, or parent-controlled escrow. Officer wants *deployed* dollars.

**Response structure.**

1. Spending reconciliation table: every dollar — date, vendor, category, invoice, bank confirmation. Cite *Matter of Hira*, 11 I&N Dec. 824 (BIA 1966).
2. Demonstrate funds cannot be readily clawed back. Closed MITA = gone in commercial sense.
3. Distinguish from "uncommitted capital": at-risk doctrine does not require zero cash on hand; it requires invested funds be committed.
4. Subtypes 2/3/4 with parent retaining control: demonstrate subsidiary's independent decision-making and absence of buy-back clause.

## 8. NOID response craft

A NOID is procedurally distinct: the officer has tentatively *decided to
deny*. The NOID is the last process due before denial. More rigorous on
three dimensions.

### 8.1 Differences from RFE

| Dimension | RFE | NOID |
|---|---|---|
| Officer's posture | "I have questions" | "I have decided to deny absent contrary evidence" |
| Response window | 87 days | 30 days |
| Evidentiary burden | Supplement | Rebut |
| Tone | Educational | Adversarial |
| Attorney supremacy | Optional | **Mandatory** |

### 8.2 Attorney supremacy gate

NOID responses are never delegated below senior associate. Lead attorney
drafts; supervising partner reviews. Client sees the filed copy, not
drafts. NOID drafting errors compound — the response becomes the record
on which any subsequent motion to reopen, appeal, or refiled petition rests.

### 8.3 Response architecture

(1) **Procedural challenge first** (where applicable). If the NOID raises a ground not in the original record or relies on extrinsic evidence, object on due-process grounds before merits. Cite 8 CFR § 103.2(b)(16)(i). (2) **Issue-by-issue rebuttal.** Quote the officer verbatim, then rebut with evidence. (3) **Re-affirm uncontested elements** so the officer need not re-find them. (4) **New evidence with affidavits** — every new exhibit gets a sworn declaration on provenance. (5) **Closing legal argument** citing INA, 8 CFR, 9 FAM, BIA precedent. NOIDs often turn on legal interpretation. (6) **Reservation of rights** — "Petitioner reserves all rights to seek further administrative or judicial review of any adverse decision."

### 8.4 NOID drafting rules

Never concede an element. Never apologize. Quote the officer's words
back, then refute. Never exceed 25 pages of argument (officer has 30
days). Tab new exhibits; never embed in the cover letter.

## 9. Interview prep (consular) — 50-question simulation

Consular interview averages 6–10 minutes. The Beneficiary must answer 50
possible questions in any order, calmly.

### 9.1 The 50 questions, organized by element

**Treaty nationality (Q1–Q5).**
1. What is your country of nationality?
2. How long have you held that nationality?
3. Do you hold any other nationality?
4. Where were you born?
5. Where do you currently reside?

**Investment / employment (Q6–Q15).**
6. What is the name of the US company?
7. What does the company do?
8. How much money have you invested?
9. Where did the money come from?
10. Walk me through how the money got from `<origin>` to `<US Petitioner>`.
11. Can the money be returned to you if the business fails?
12. How much of the company do you own? (Subtypes 1/2)
13. Who are the other owners?
14. Are they nationals of the treaty country?
15. (Subtypes 3/4) Who employs you and what is your role?

**Real and operating + marginality (Q16–Q25).**
16. Where is the business located?
17. Have you visited the premises?
18. How many employees does the business have?
19. Are they full-time? Are they on payroll?
20. What was the business's revenue last year?
21. What does it project for the next year? Five years?
22. Who are your customers?
23. Who are your suppliers?
24. Why does this business need to be in the United States?
25. Could you do this business from `<treaty country>` instead? (Trick: answer "yes" only if accurate; explain why US is the chosen market.)

**Develop and direct OR essential skill (Q26–Q35).**
26. What is your role in the business?
27. Who reports to you?
28. Whom do you report to?
29. What decisions do you make?
30. What decisions do you NOT make?
31. (Subtype 4) What is the specific skill you bring?
32. (Subtype 4) Why can't a US worker do your job?
33. (Subtype 4) How long did you train to develop this skill?
34. (Subtype 3) How many people do you supervise?
35. (Subtype 3) What is your decision-making authority over budget / hiring / strategy?

**Nonimmigrant intent (Q36–Q42).**
36. Do you intend to live permanently in the United States?
37. Where will you live during your E-2 stay?
38. Do you own property in `<treaty country>`?
39. Do you have family in `<treaty country>`?
40. What ties do you maintain to `<treaty country>`?
41. Do you have a return plan when E-2 ends?
42. Have you ever applied for a green card?

**Procedural / family (Q43–Q50).**
43. Have you been to the United States before? When?
44. Have you ever been refused a US visa?
45. Have you ever been arrested?
46. Are you bringing your family?
47. What does your spouse do?
48. Are your children enrolled in US schools?
49. When do you plan to depart the US at the end of your E-2?
50. Is there anything else you want me to know?

### 9.2 Demeanor coaching

Eye contact. Answer only what was asked — never volunteer. Ask politely
for clarification rather than guess. Specific numbers, specific dates —
"about a year ago" loses; "March 2025" wins. Say "I don't know" rather
than speculate. Calm pace; officers read panic as concealment.

### 9.3 What to bring vs what NOT to volunteer

**Bring** (single folder, in order): passport; DS-160 confirmation;
DS-156E confirmation; appointment letter; original petition packet
(returning) OR one-page case summary (new); civil documents requested.

**Do NOT volunteer**: the full petition packet (officer has it);
unasked personal achievements; future-plan speculation; anything
contradicting the DS-160. Re-read DS-160 the night before — answers
must match.

### 9.4 Mock interview discipline

Two mocks per case. Mock 1 at Day 50 (catches gross gaps). Mock 2 at
Day 58 (3–7 days before real interview). Both recorded with consent,
reviewed jointly, written delta between them.

## 10. Approval handling + renewal calendar

### 10.1 The closing letter

Within 5 business days of approval (USCIS) or visa issuance (consular),
firm sends a closing letter containing: (1) approval notice / visa stamp
scan; (2) validity period (ISO 8601); (3) I-94 retrieved from
`i94.cbp.dhs.gov`; (4) travel advisory for next and subsequent entries;
(5) renewal calendar (§ 10.2); (6) reporting obligations — AR-11
address change, employment, ownership, dependent status; (7) firm
escalation contacts.

### 10.2 Renewal calendar (set at approval, not at expiration)

| Trigger | Action |
|---|---|
| **Approval date + 1 day** | Calendar reminders: 6 months, 4 months, 2 months, 1 month before expiration |
| **Expiration − 6 months** | Outreach to client: confirm intent to renew; refresh intake on changed facts |
| **Expiration − 4 months** | Begin renewal build (60-day standard if facts unchanged; 90-day if material change) |
| **Expiration − 2 months** | Filing-ready packet on shelf; election decision (premium yes/no) |
| **Expiration − 1 month** | File. Earlier if premium; later only with cause |
| **Expiration − 0** | Status lapses if not extended. Hard stop. |

> **Whitfield, renewal triage rule.** If the client surfaces at
> expiration − 60 days for a renewal of a 24-month grant, file
> consular at the home post — do not chase USCIS extension on a
> compressed clock.

## 11. Sub-type-specific practical adjustments

The methodology above is the universal scaffold. Each sub-type shifts
the load in characteristic ways.

### 11.1 Subtype 1 — individual investor

**Where the file lives or dies.** SOF chain visibility and W-2 evidence.

1. **SOF chain visible.** One-page table at front of Tab E. Five rows — origin, receipt, conversion, wire, deployment — each pinned to an exhibit. The officer never asks "where did the money come from?"
2. **Tapu defensive paragraph.** Mandatory for any Turkish real estate in chain. Place at first Tapu citation in Section IV. Explain registry-based title, the Tapu Müdürlüğü's role, why the document looks unfamiliar to a US adjudicator.
3. **TÜFE escalation footnote.** Mandatory for rental income spanning > 24 months in high-inflation currency. CPI-adjusted alongside nominal. Translator's note required.
4. **Marginality W-2 evidence.** Two non-Beneficiary W-2s minimum. Fewer = not yet fileable; discuss with client.
5. **Subtype 1 exemplar (US LLC, Q1 2026 filing) demonstrated this pattern by** building the SOF chain in 8 days from intake, pre-empting the predictable Tapu RFE with a 6-paragraph defensive block, and approving on regular processing without RFE.

### 11.2 Subtype 2 — corporate-owned investor

**Where the file lives or dies.** Audited-financials currency match against I-129E investment amount, plus board-resolution amount-match.

1. **Audited financials currency.** Parent's audited balance sheet shows, in the parent's reporting currency, the deployed funds. I-129E USD figure must reconcile within $100 at wire-date FX. Prepare a one-page reconciliation memo as Exhibit F.X.
2. **Board resolution amount-match.** The Yönetim Kurulu Kararı authorizing the transfer must state exact amount and exact recipient. Drift triggers automatic RFE.
3. **Apostille/legalization queue.** Foreign corporate documents apostilled (Hague) or consular-legalized. Begin at intake, never week 8.
4. **Translation discipline.** 8 CFR § 103.2(b)(3). Every foreign-language document gets certified translation with translator's competency declaration.

### 11.3 Subtype 3 — executive / supervisory employee

**Where the file lives or dies.** Role enumeration, compensation benchmarking, prior P&L responsibility evidence.

1. **Role enumeration.** Section VII of cover letter lists, by name, the decisions the Beneficiary will make: hiring, budget, signing, strategic — with dollar thresholds.
2. **Anti-routine-work paragraph.** 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(a) excludes employees doing "routine substantive staff work" with incidental supervision. Affirmatively exclude: "The Beneficiary's role is not staff work supplemented by supervision; supervision and policy direction are the role itself."
3. **Compensation benchmarking.** BLS/industry data matching proposed comp to executive tier by industry and geography. Tab H.
4. **Prior P&L evidence.** CV is not enough. Letters from prior employers, signed by HR or CEO, attesting to prior P&L scope, headcount, decision authority.
5. **Span-of-control org chart.** Tab H. Visual showing Beneficiary's position, reports, dotted lines.

### 11.4 Subtype 4 — essential skills employee

**Where the file lives or dies.** Specialized-knowledge demonstration, salary-differential service record, pre-employment offer-letter sequencing.

1. **Specialized-knowledge framing.** 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(18). Skill must be (a) specialized, (b) essential, (c) not readily available in US labor market. Three exhibits per leg: credentials + technical descriptions; enterprise's documented dependence (specs, project catalogues); US labor market data on skill scarcity.
2. **Salary-differential service record.** Foreign Sicil showing prior foreign salary; US compensation offer; differential explained — when US tier matches expectation, simple; when not, cost-of-living math and total comp.
3. **Pre-employment offer-letter sequencing.** Job offer must pre-date I-129 filing. Letter dated *after* filing = automatic RFE. Counsel reviews draft *before* signature to catch role-description drift from cover letter.
4. **Time-limited essentiality framing.** Short-term skill (training US staff and transitioning out) framed honestly with duration estimate. Do not over-claim permanence.
5. **Skills-not-readily-available paragraph.** Mandatory. Cite 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(b) and exclude foreign-language-and-culture-alone (statutorily insufficient).
6. **Subtype 4 exemplar (US subsidiary, energy sector, Q1 2024 filing) demonstrated this pattern by** sequencing the job-offer letter 21 days before I-129 filing, layering the salary-differential exhibit alongside the foreign service record, and framing the medium-voltage-sales specialization as durably essential to a multi-year US deployment — approved without RFE.

## 12. Closing margin notes

Practitioner reminders, kept by my desk:

1. The cover letter is a tour, not a brief — every paragraph points at an exhibit.
2. Build outward from SOF — if E fails, nothing else matters.
3. W-2s win marginality. Projections do not.
4. Premium is a calendar, not a strategy.
5. NOIDs are won by quoting the officer; RFEs by delivering the document the officer asked for, fast.
6. Mock the interview twice — Day 50 and Day 58.
7. Renewal calendar at approval, not at expiration.
8. Defensive paragraphs in the letter; evidence in the tabs. Never the other way.
9. Read the file as the officer — if you cannot tell in 90 seconds who the principal investor is and where the money came from, neither can the officer.
