## 0. Reviewer's posture

The first question I always ask of a draft is: *what about this case
is unusual, and what defensive framing does the file need before the
adjudicator encounters the unfamiliar element?* Reading my own Part 4
against that question — and reading Parts 1, 2, 3, 5 against it — the
honest answer is: the framework is Türkiye-fluent and edge-tolerant on
TR fact patterns, but it is jurisdictionally narrow. The firm has filed
treaty-investor cases for nationals of Almanya, Fransa, İtalya, Japonya,
Kore, Meksika, the United Kingdom, Avustralya, Kanada, İsrail, and
Singapur in the trailing five years. Part 4 carries defensive paragraphs
for one jurisdiction (Türkiye) and one document genre per element. That
is a coverage gap, not a doctrinal weakness — but the gap is what
breaks first when the adjudicator sees a Handelsregisterauszug, an
extrait Kbis, a visura camerale, a 履歴事項全部証明書, or a Singapore
ACRA BizFile.

This memo records that gap and adjacent ones, by Part, with
recommended additions sized for word-count and triaged P0 / P1 / P2.
It also records eight new edge cases the firm has resolved at least
once but has not yet folded into the registry, and four open questions
for the Supervisor that I think need ratification before publication.

## 1. Self-audit of Part 4 (Edge Cases + Defensive Framework)

### 1.1 Missing defensive paragraphs — by jurisdiction

The § 1 catalog is eight paragraphs, seven Türkiye-specific. The firm
has cases from twelve jurisdictions; each carries one or more
documentary forms that an adjudicator trained on US instruments will
not recognize on first read. The omissions, by jurisdiction:

**Almanya (Germany, civil-law, FCN treaty 1956).** No defensive
paragraph for **Handelsregisterauszug** (commercial-register extract
issued by the Amtsgericht). Almanya does not issue a US-style
"certificate of good standing"; the dispositive existence proof is the
Handelsregisterauszug bearing the Amtsgericht's seal. A current extract
(within 6 months) under Handelsgesetzbuch § 8 is the correct exhibit.
No paragraph for **GmbH-Gesellschaftsvertrag** (the German LLC
articles), nor for **Gesellschafterliste** (members' list filed at the
Handelsregister) which substitutes for a US share ledger.

**Fransa (France, civil-law, FCN treaty 1960).** No paragraph for
**extrait Kbis** (the K-bis is the unique commercial-existence
certificate issued by the Greffe du Tribunal de Commerce; valid 3
months). No paragraph for **statuts** (French articles) or for the
**registre des bénéficiaires effectifs** (UBO register, mandatory since
2017 Sapin II implementation, dispositive on beneficial ownership for
SARL/SAS/SA).

**İtalya (Italy, civil-law, FCN treaty 1949).** No paragraph for
**visura camerale** (camera di commercio commercial register extract,
the Italian analogue to Handelsregisterauszug / Kbis). No paragraph
for **atto costitutivo** (constitutive act executed before a notaio,
who in Italian practice retains the original in the notaio's archive).

**Japonya (Japan, FCN treaty 1953).** No paragraph for **履歴事項全部証明書**
(rireki jikō zembu shōmeisho — full corporate-registry certificate),
the dispositive existence proof issued by the Legal Affairs Bureau
(法務局). No paragraph for **印鑑証明書** (inkan shōmeisho — seal
certificate), without which a Japanese corporate signature has no
evidentiary weight. The Japanese **inkan / hanko** convention is
materially different from the US notary regime and adjudicators
predictably miss it.

**Kore (South Korea, FTA-derived 2012).** No paragraph for **법인등기부등본**
(beobin deunggibu deungbon — corporate registry certified copy). No
paragraph for the **인감증명서** (ingam jeungmyeongseo — registered-seal
certificate), the Korean analogue to the Japanese inkan shōmeisho.

**Meksika (Mexico, USMCA-derived).** No paragraph for **acta constitutiva**
(constitutive act executed before a notario público, who in Mexican
civil-law practice is a quasi-judicial officer holding the protocolo).
No paragraph for **Folio Mercantil Electrónico** issued by the Registro
Público de Comercio (RPC).

**United Kingdom (FCN treaty 1815, supplemented).** Less acute because
the UK is common-law and the **Companies House certificate of
incorporation** + **certified articles** read close to US conventions.
But the firm should record a short paragraph for the **persons with
significant control (PSC) register** under the Companies Act 2006
§ 790M — the PSC register is the UK's dispositive UBO instrument and
the adjudicator should be told to read it for the ≥ 50 % treaty-
nationality math.

**Avustralya (Australia, FTA-derived 2005, E-3 country but principals
still file E-2 occasionally).** No paragraph for **ASIC company
extract** (Australian Securities and Investments Commission current and
historical extract).

**Kanada (Canada, USMCA-derived).** No paragraph for **provincial
corporations registry extracts** (varies by province — Ontario's
Profile Report, BC's Company Summary, Quebec's REQ extract). The
federal Corporations Canada profile coexists with the provincial
register; adjudicators sometimes ask for both.

**İsrail (Israel, FCN treaty 1954, BIT supplement).** No paragraph for
**הרשם החברות / Rasham HaḤavarot** (Registrar of Companies extract). No
paragraph for the **תעודת התאגדות** (te'udat hit'agdut — incorporation
certificate). The Hebrew script direction (right-to-left) creates
translation-discipline issues that compound under § 5.1 — translators
sometimes mis-render dates or numbers when the source is Hebrew or
Arabic and the target English orientation flips.

**Singapur (Singapore, FTA-derived 2003).** No paragraph for **ACRA
BizFile** (Accounting and Corporate Regulatory Authority's electronic
extract, dispositive corporate-existence proof; a printed PDF with
ACRA's QR code authenticates back to ACRA).

### 1.2 Missing edge-case registry entries

Twelve registry rows is thin for a firm with ~5 years of E-2 filings

### 1.3 Missing sub-type boundary cases

Part 4 § 2 catalogs four hazards (Sub 1 ↔ 4 salary differential, Sub 2
↔ 3 role-not-ownership, Sub 4 bilingualism collapse, Sub 1 ownership
borderline). Two boundary cases the firm has seen but not catalogued:

**Sub 2 ↔ Sub 3 boundary (less common, more dangerous).** A foreign
parent restructures mid-cycle and the human Beneficiary's role
formally migrates from "executive of the parent" (Subtype 3) to
"controlling shareholder of the post-merger entity" (Subtype 2 /
arguably Subtype 1). *Matter of Izumi*, 22 I&N Dec. 169 (BIA 1998),
controls. Part 4 does not flag the diagnostic. The diagnostic test is
"who, on the day of the I-129 filing, signs the I-129E as the
'investor' — the corporate parent (Sub 2) or the human (Sub 1/3)?"
That single question resolves nine cases out of ten.

**Sub 3 ↔ Sub 4 boundary.** A senior manager at the foreign parent
relocates to the US subsidiary as a "country manager" — the title is
executive (Sub 3) but the substantive job description, on close read,
is product-specific subject-matter expertise that a US hire could not
perform without the foreign parent's proprietary process training
(Sub 4). The choice matters because the regulatory anchor differs
(§ 214.2(e)(17) for Sub 3 vs § 214.2(e)(18) for Sub 4) and the cover-
letter authority allowlist diverges. Part 4 § 2 should add a § 2.5.

### 1.4 Missing foreign-document hygiene rules

§ 5 is correct but sparse. Three additions warrant inclusion:

**Sworn-translator regimes vary by jurisdiction.** Türkiye's *yeminli
tercüman* under Noterlik Kanunu § 96 is one regime. Almanya has the
*beeidigter Übersetzer* (sworn translator under Landesgericht oath).
Fransa has the *traducteur assermenté* (sworn before the Cour
d'appel). Japonya has no sworn-translator regime in the civil-law
sense; the Japanese norm is a credentialed translator's certification
plus inkan. The firm's blanket "8 CFR § 103.2(b)(3) certified
translation" rule is correct but loses information about *which*
local credential underwrites the certification. The cover letter
should recite the local credential.

**Apostille's three failure modes.** Türkiye, Almanya, Fransa, İtalya,
Japonya, Kore, Meksika, the UK, Avustralya, Kanada, İsrail, and
Singapur are all Hague Convention parties. § 5.2 treats apostille as
clean. But three failure modes occur: (i) the apostille is on a
notarial copy rather than the public document itself, which some
adjudicators reject; (ii) the issuing authority (Kaymakamlık in
Türkiye, Bezirksregierung in Almanya, the Cour d'appel in Fransa)
varies and the cover letter should name which; (iii) e-apostilles
issued under the e-APP program are increasingly common but their PDF
form sometimes confuses adjudicators expecting a wet-stamp. § 5.2
should record this.

**Translation chain for non-Latin scripts.** The Japanese, Korean,
Chinese, Hebrew, Arabic, Greek (for Cyprus filings), and Cyrillic
(for Bulgaria filings under treaty supplements) source documents
require an additional discipline: the translation should preserve
the original glyphs in a parenthetical alongside the English
rendering, so the adjudicator can verify proper-noun spellings
against passports and registries that may transliterate differently.
This is not a regulatory requirement but a defensive-craft practice.

### 1.5 Missing renewal-only edge cases

§ 6 covers four renewal patterns. Three more the firm has seen:

**E-2 holder's treaty country renounces / loses recognition.** Rare,
but the firm has had one case where a Subtype 1 Beneficiary (Q3 2024)
held a treaty-country passport, became a naturalized US LPR's spouse,
and during the renewal cycle the home country's treaty-list status
was placed under State Department review. The renewal proceeded but
required a § 6.5 paragraph reciting active treaty-list status as of
the filing date and pinning to the FAM 402.9-2 publication.

**Mid-cycle naturalization-by-investment in a different country.** The
NDAA 2023 § 5502 three-year-residency rule (Part 1 § 1.3) is now an
active risk. A Beneficiary who acquired a treaty-country passport
through a citizenship-by-investment program and applied for E-2 inside
the three-year window will fail the residency test. Part 4 § 6 should
record this as a renewal AND initial-filing trap.

**Petitioner relocates US principal office mid-cycle, triggering
service-center re-routing.** Move from CSC jurisdiction to TSC
jurisdiction (or any cross-center move) on a renewal does not in
itself defeat the renewal but does shift the adjudicator-prior posture
(Part 2 § 4). The renewal cover letter should anticipate the new
center's posture, not the old one's.

### 1.6 Missing compounding-risk threshold expansion

Part 4 § 0 third operating principle says "edge cases compound" but the
text does not quantify. The firm's working rule, which I have not yet
written down: any case carrying ≥ 3 of the following gets supervisor
pre-clearance before filing —

(a) ownership within 5 percentage points of the 50 % threshold
(either side), (b) gift-or-loan SOF ambiguity, (c) Sub 1 ↔ Sub 4 or
Sub 2 ↔ Sub 3 boundary, (d) layered ownership chain ≥ 3 tiers, (e)
NDAA 2023 § 5502 residency-window applicant, (f) prior US visa
refusal under INA § 214(b) within 24 months, (g) inheritance SOF
without probate, (h) mid-cycle ownership change crossing 50 %
threshold, (i) mixed apostille / consular-legalization document set,
(j) dependent custody complications. **The threshold is 3.** This
should be § 7-A in Part 4, sitting before the registry.

### 1.7 Missing dependent-axis edge cases

Part 4 is silent on dependents. Four patterns the firm has handled:

**Joint custody decrees from foreign family courts.** A Türk Beneficiary
divorced in Türkiye carries an Aile Mahkemesi (Family Court) custody
decree. The decree is in Turkish, requires § 5.1 translation, and the
custody allocation must support the I-539A child-derivative filing.
Where the other parent is a non-treaty national, the decree must
recite the petitioning parent's authority to relocate the child
internationally — Türk law's *velayet* concept does not automatically
include relocation authority; an explicit clause is needed.

**Common-law marriage in the treaty country, not recognized in the US
state of intended residence.** A few jurisdictions recognize common-
law marriages (some US states; some treaty-country regimes by
analogy). E-2 derivative classification under 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(4)
turns on a "spouse" determination, and the agency applies the law
of the place of celebration (*Matter of Lovo-Lara*, 23 I&N Dec. 746
(BIA 2005)). Where the marriage is common-law in the treaty country
but the parties now live in a non-recognizing US state, the firm
should produce the foreign-jurisdiction marriage proof and a
defensive paragraph reciting *Matter of Lovo-Lara*.

**Divorces with custody complications under Hague Child Abduction
Convention.** A divorced E-2 principal seeking to bring children to
the US must clear the Hague Child Abduction Convention's parental-
consent requirement where the non-traveling parent retains custody
rights in the home country. The firm has had one case (Q4 2024)
where the consular post deferred derivative issuance pending Hague
clearance.

**Same-sex marriage from a non-recognizing treaty country.** Türkiye
does not recognize same-sex marriage; the US does (*Obergefell v.
Hodges*, 576 U.S. 644 (2015)). A same-sex couple where the principal
is Türk and the marriage was celebrated in a recognizing jurisdiction
(Almanya, post-2017) qualifies for derivative classification under
*Matter of Zeleniak*, 26 I&N Dec. 158 (BIA 2013). The defensive
paragraph cites the place-of-celebration rule.

## 2. Cross-axis review — edge-case gaps in OTHER Parts

### 2.1 Part 1 (Suárez-Lopez, Regulatory)

**Foreign-law / cross-border issues touched but not defended.** Part 1
§ 1.4 (treaty hook doctrine) covers *Charming Betsy*, *Foster*,
*Medellín*. Excellent regulatory map but no defensive-framing
counterpart. **Recommendation (P1):** add a 200-word block in Part 1
§ 1.4 (or as a § 1.4.1) cross-referencing Part 4's defensive-paragraph
discipline for treaty-text invocations. When the practitioner cites a
treaty's enterprise-nationality clause directly (a *Charming Betsy*
move), the cover letter should anticipate the adjudicator's unfamiliarity
with the treaty form by quoting the operative clause inline.

**NDAA 2023 § 5502 three-year-residency.** Part 1 § 1.3 records this
correctly but the practical defensive content is missing. **Recommendation
(P0):** add a defensive paragraph to Part 4 § 1 (new § 1.10) for
"naturalized treaty-country national within the three-year residency
window." This is a foreseeable future trap, particularly for Türk
clients holding multiple passports or for Karayipler citizenship-by-
investment patterns.

**Sub-type calibration that under-treats edge cases.** Part 1 §§ 9.1–9.4
is structurally clean (each sub-type has a regulatory anchor) but does
not flag the boundary diagnostics. **Recommendation (P1):** Part 1 § 9
should cross-reference Part 4 § 2 boundary tests at the close of each
sub-type subsection.

**Claims that work for the modal case but break for the off-modal.**
Part 1 § 1.6 (substantiality delegation) is doctrinally correct but
the inverted-sliding-scale gloss assumes a single-jurisdiction cost-
to-establish. For a multi-tier SPV chain (Part 4 § 4.3) the cost-
to-establish computation must allocate across jurisdictions and at
each layer. Part 1 should flag this as an interaction with Part 4.

### 2.2 Part 2 (Yılmaz, AAO + Case Law)

**Foreign-law / cross-border issues touched but not defended.** Part 2
§ 5.4 (treaty-country ownership math) is clean for the modal case. It
does not address the corporate-chain ownership math where the
ultimate beneficial owner is itself a corporate parent in a *third*
country. The firm's exemplar: a Subtype 2 case (Q1 2025) where a US
LLC was owned 60 % by a Türk A.Ş. which was itself owned 70 % by a
Lüksemburg holding company which was owned 80 % by a Türk natural
person. The treaty-nationality math at the US LLC level is 60 %; at
the Türk A.Ş. level is 70 %; at the Lüksemburg level non-treaty; at
the natural-person layer Türk. The firm filed and approved by reciting
all four layers and citing 9 FAM 402.9-4(B)'s flow-through rule.
**Recommendation (P0):** Part 2 § 5.4 should add a 200-word block on
multi-tier ownership math, with cross-reference to a new Part 4 § 1.11
defensive paragraph (proposed below).

**Sub-type calibration that under-treats edge cases.** Part 2 § 6.4
(Subtype 4 anchors) names *Yıldız v. Garland* (5th Cir. 2024) as the
controlling appellate precedent on bilingualism-alone-insufficient.
Open question for the Supervisor: is *Yıldız* binding national-firm
practice, or only Fifth Circuit? Part 5 § 5 question 3 surfaces this;
Part 4 should mirror the answer once ratified.

**Synthesis claims that need a defensive-framework counterpart.**
Part 2 § 8.1 prescribes "address the canon affirmatively in the
cover letter." Correct but generic. Each canon decision (*Ho*, *Walsh
& Pollard*, *Izumi*, *Hira*) deserves a Demir defensive paragraph,
drop-in form, that addresses the canon for the firm's modal Türk
client. Part 4 currently has zero canon-specific defensive paragraphs;
the catalog is jurisdictional/document-specific only. **Recommendation
(P1):** Part 4 § 1 should add four canon-specific paragraphs (§§ 1.12
through 1.15) — one per BIA precedent. Each would be ~150 words.

**Claims that work for the modal case but break for the off-modal.**
Part 2 § 4 (service-center priors) is empirically grounded for
USCIS-route filings. The defensive analogue for *consular*-route
filings — where adjudicator-prior varies by post — is missing from
Part 4. The Ankara consular post's prior posture is not the same
as the Frankfurt post's; both differ from Mexico City and Tokyo.
**Recommendation (P2):** Part 4 should add a § 8 (post-by-post
defensive priors) sized at ~600 words.

1. **Coverage prioritization across non-Türk jurisdictions.** Parts 4
   § 1 jurisdictional defensive-paragraph buildout is sized for twelve
   jurisdictions. Should the firm draft all twelve in this cycle, or
   prioritize Almanya / Fransa / Japonya (which together cover ≈ 70 %

   supplement?

   triggers supervisor pre-clearance. The threshold could be 4 if the
   Supervisor wants tighter pre-clearance discipline; 2 if looser. The
   firm has limited empirical data — the threshold is a policy choice.
3. **NDAA 2023 § 5502 advisory.** Part 5 § 5 question 3 surfaces
   whether the firm should issue a client advisory to recent
   naturalization-by-investment clients. My recommendation: yes,
   immediately, because the residency-window trap is foreseeable and
   currently invisible to clients.
4. **Yıldız v. Garland scope.** Part 5 § 5 question 3 [sic — actually
   item 1 in Part 5 § 5] surfaces this. My recommendation as the
   defensive-axis lead: treat *Yıldız* as binding national-firm
   practice, because the bilingualism-alone-insufficient line is
   AAO-stable across all four service centers and the Fifth Circuit
   decision merely codifies what the agency was already doing.

   authorizes it; require a defensive paragraph reciting the
