## 0. Posture and method

The four lenses are independently drafted; this memo reads each
through the AAO + case-law filter and asks: where do empirical
priors, BIA precedent, federal court doctrine, or AAO non-precedent
patterns belong but are not cited? I limit comment to gaps that are
material from the case-law lens — i.e., gaps that would materially
shift either approval probability or appellate posture if cured. I
do not chase decoration. Where a gap can be cured by a 50-word
defensive paragraph, I say so and stop; where it requires a
sub-section addition, I size it.

A note on the empirical claims that follow. They draw on the firm's
internal AAO non-precedent dataset (n ≈ 380 redacted decisions, 2021
through Q1 2026) per Part 2 § 0. Where I quote percentages without
verification to a public AAO release, I mark them `[per the firm's
empirical observation, ~XX% range]` per house convention.

## 1. Self-audit of Part 2 (AAO + Case Law)

The Supervisor's instruction is gap analysis, not pat-on-back. Part 2
covers the four BIA precedents, the AAO denial-pattern empirical
study, federal court appeals, service-center priors, and the
sub-type case-law anchors. It is solid on what it covers. Six gaps
are material.

### 1.1 BIA precedent canon is too narrow

Part 2 § 1 treats *Ho*, *Walsh & Pollard*, *Izumi*, *Hira* as the
"four BIA precedents that drive nearly every denial rationale." The
list is correct as the *modal* canon but undercounts decisions that
the AAO cites by analogy. Three precedents missing entirely:

(i) *Matter of Lin*, 16 I&N Dec. 60 (BIA 1976) — although an L-1
decision, *Lin* articulates the "specialized knowledge" framework
that the AAO imports by analogy into Subtype 4 essential-skills
adjudication. The bilingualism-alone-is-insufficient line owes its
analytical structure to *Lin*'s distinction between "general
familiarity" and "advanced" knowledge. Five Subtype 4 denials in the
firm's 2023–2025 dataset cite *Lin* expressly [per the firm's
empirical observation, ~5–8% of Sub-4 denials, flag for Supervisor].

(ii) *Matter of Soriano*, 19 I&N Dec. 764 (BIA 1988) — addresses
the burden-of-proof allocation under INA § 291 (8 U.S.C. § 1361).
The decision is cited routinely in AAO non-precedent decisions
articulating that the petitioner bears the burden on every element
"by a preponderance of the evidence." Part 2 does not state the
preponderance standard at all. This is a non-trivial omission: the
firm's cover letter should affirmatively recite the *Soriano*
preponderance standard so the adjudicator is reminded that the bar
is preponderance, not clear-and-convincing.

(iii) *Matter of Chawathe*, 25 I&N Dec. 369 (AAO 2010) — though
nominally an N-470 decision, *Chawathe* is the modern locus
classicus for the preponderance standard in nonimmigrant petitions.
The AAO cites *Chawathe* in roughly 30–40% of its nonimmigrant
non-precedent decisions [per the firm's empirical observation].
Part 2 does not mention it.

### 1.2 The AAO non-precedent reading-room releases are
under-represented

Part 2 § 2 presents the empirical denial-pattern table at aggregate
level. What it does *not* do is cross-reference specific AAO
non-precedent reading-room releases by their published file
identifiers (the format `MAT-2024-XXXXX` or, for older releases,
the AAO file-number redactions). The firm's analyst-facing memo
should be able to point a junior associate at the *specific*
released decision that exemplifies each rationale. Three sub-cells
are particularly thin:

- *Subtype 2 ownership-chain disclosure denials at NSC.* Part 2 § 2.2
  reports NSC as mid-volume with Subtype 2 corporate-ownership math
  drawing scrutiny. The dataset has only ~9 NSC Subtype 2 decisions;
  the year-over-year ranges should not be cited in client memos
  without flagging the small-sample noise.
- *Subtype 3 executive-vs-functional at TSC.* The dataset has ~6 TSC
  Subtype 3 decisions, all denials. The 100% denial rate is almost
  certainly small-sample noise; Part 2 should explicitly disclose
  the n.
- *Subtype 4 specialized-knowledge sufficiency at VSC.* The dataset
  has ~14 VSC Sub-4 decisions; the inferences are weaker than the
  table presentation suggests.

The fix is a **cell-by-cell n disclosure** in the empirical tables —
add a column "n" alongside the denial-range column.

### 1.3 Federal-court appellate decisions are under-listed

Part 2 § 3 catalogs three federal-court decisions: *Hashemi*,
*Tekin*, *Yıldız*. The list is too short. The firm's litigation
posture requires awareness of:

(i) *Saavedra Bruno v. Albright*, 197 F.3d 1153 (D.C. Cir. 1999),
which Part 2 cites correctly for consular nonreviewability but
should be set in context with *Allen v. Milas*, 896 F.3d 1094
(9th Cir. 2018), which extended the doctrine to the E-2 context
specifically. *Allen* is the operative 9th Circuit articulation.

(ii) *Department of State v. Muñoz*, 144 S. Ct. 1812 (2024) — the
Supreme Court's 2024 reaffirmation of consular nonreviewability,
extending it against constitutional-claim wrappers. Part 2 does not
cite *Muñoz*. The decision materially shapes the firm's
litigation-posture calculus for consular-route denials going
forward.

(iii) The *Garland v. Ming Dai* line on substantial-evidence review,
141 S. Ct. 1669 (2021) — articulates the substantial-evidence
standard as applied to factual findings on judicial review. Cited
by analogy in APA challenges to USCIS-route denials.

(iv) The trailing-twelve-months D.D.C. and E.D. Va. district court
docket. Part 2 mentions "the dominant USCIS-review fora" but does
not name specific 2024–2026 decisions. The firm should maintain a
running citation list; my running list includes (with verification
flags): *Şahin v. Mayorkas* (D.D.C. 2025) (slip op., redacted) on
substantiality; *Morales-García v. USCIS* (E.D. Va. 2025) (redacted)
on at-risk; and a 2024 E.D. Va. decision remanding a Subtype 4
denial on inadequate consideration of submitted comparable-salary
evidence. **Citation verification flag for Supervisor — these are
slip-opinion redactions in the firm's litigation tracker; confirm
each before public-facing use.**

### 1.4 Denial-rationale templates need explicit enumeration

Part 2 § 5 ("Common AAO rationales for denial") catalogs five
templates — substantiality below threshold, marginality unsupported,
develop-and-direct insufficient, treaty-country ownership math,
at-risk failure. The firm's empirical experience supports a longer
list. Eight to twelve stable templates exist. Missing from § 5:

- *Source-of-funds chain incomplete* (distinct from "at-risk
  failure"; cited in roughly 12–18% of substantiality RFEs in the
  firm's dataset [flag for Supervisor]).
- *Business plan credibility* under *Ho* (cited as the standalone
  rationale in roughly 8–14% of denials, often combined with
  marginality).
- *Sub-4 specialized-knowledge insufficiency* — though Part 2 § 6.4
  treats it under sub-type, it deserves enumeration as a standalone
  template with its own evidentiary fix.
- *Sub-3 executive-vs-functional* — same comment.
- *Treaty-country ownership chain disclosure* — distinct from the
  ownership math; this template attacks the *evidence* of ownership
  rather than the math.
- *Maintenance-of-status* (renewal-only) — the AAO denies extensions
  where the petitioner failed to maintain qualifying conditions
  during the prior period. Part 2 does not address this template at
  all, though it is the most common renewal-denial rationale.

The fix: re-write § 5 to enumerate 10 templates (the existing 5 plus
the 5 above), each with the typical AAO language, the empirical
frequency, and the evidentiary fix.

### 1.5 Service-center prior tables are too coarse

Part 2 § 4 presents service-center priors at aggregate level. The
practitioner needs sub-type-by-sub-type tables. A CSC Subtype 1
filing has materially different priors than a CSC Subtype 4 filing,
and the aggregate table conceals the difference. The fix: replace
§ 4's prose with a 4×4 table (service center × sub-type) showing
denial range and dominant RFE template per cell. Where n is too
small to support a range, mark "n insufficient — flag for
Supervisor."

### 1.6 Post-2024 rulemaking and Notices need explicit treatment

Part 2 does not address post-2024 administrative developments that
affect adjudicator behavior:

(i) The 2024 USCIS fee final rule (89 Fed. Reg. 6194 (Jan. 31,
2024)) — restructured I-129 and premium-processing fees; affects
the premium-processing calculus in Part 2 § 8.3.

(ii) NDAA 2023 § 5502 implementation cable (22 STATE 130543, Dec.
2022, with subsequent updates) — Part 1 addresses the residency
requirement; Part 2 should address its effect on AAO appeals
(several denials in 2024–2025 turn on the three-year residency
showing).

(iii) The 2025 USCIS Policy Manual update to Vol. 2, Pt. F, Ch. 6
(if released; flag for Supervisor — confirm currency) tightening
substantiality documentation expectations.

The fix: a new Part 2 § 9 ("Recent administrative developments
affecting adjudicator behavior") of ~400 words.

## 2. Cross-axis review — case-law claims missing from other Parts

### 2.1 Part 1 (Suárez-Lopez · Regulatory)

Part 1 is exhaustive on statute, regulation, FAM, and the post-*Loper
Bright* deference architecture. It is appropriately thin on AAO
non-precedent decisions because that is Part 2's territory. Three
case-law-anchor gaps are nonetheless material.

(i) **Section 2.12 (at-risk; impermissible loans).** The doctrinal
discussion runs through 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(12) and notes that "the
doctrinal lineage runs to *Matter of Walsh & Pollard*, treated by
Prof. Yılmaz in Part 2." The anchor decision for at-risk is *Hira*,
not *Walsh & Pollard*; *Walsh & Pollard* is the marginality anchor.
The misattribution should be corrected, and the *Hira* pin-cite at
11 I&N Dec. 824, 828–30 should appear inline.

(ii) **Section 2.15 (marginality).** The five-year horizon is
attributed to "regulatory practice rather than statutory text" with
a parenthetical reference to *Ho*. The five-year horizon is more
specifically the AAO's adoption of *Ho*'s EB-5 framing into E-2 by
analogy through a stable line of non-precedent decisions; the cite
should walk through that lineage. A 200-word addition to § 2.15
would close the gap.

(iii) **Section 7.3 (post-Loper Bright pressure points).** Part 1
identifies three pressure points (substantiality, develop-and-direct,
essential skills multi-factor) but does not catalog the federal
decisions that have begun to test them. As of 2026-04-29 no
successful federal challenge to the FAM substantiality construction
has occurred (Part 1 acknowledges this), but the firm should be
tracking the dockets where the challenges might surface. *Şahin v.
Mayorkas* (D.D.C. 2025, slip op.) is one such docket; the firm's
litigation tracker should be cross-referenced. **Citation
verification flag for Supervisor.**

(iv) **Section 7.4 (Auer / Kisor).** The discussion is doctrinally
correct but does not engage the AAO non-precedent pattern. The AAO,
when interpreting its own regulations under (e)(17) "ultimate
control" and (e)(18) "essential to the efficient operation," is
itself an "agency interpretation of the agency's regulation"
candidate for *Kisor* deference on judicial review. A 150-word
addition explaining how *Kisor* operates on AAO interpretive
decisions would complete the doctrinal map.

### 2.2 Part 3 (Whitfield · Practical)

Part 3 cites BIA precedent thinly and only at three discrete points:
*Walsh & Pollard* in § 7.3 (marginality RFE), *Izumi* in § 7.4
(50%-exact ownership), *Hira* in § 7.6 (at-risk RFE). The thinness
is partly appropriate — Part 3 is methodology, not doctrine — but
the cover-letter and RFE-response templates would carry materially
more weight if the case-law spine were exposed at three additional
points.

(i) **§ 1.5 (source of funds intake question).** Q24–Q29 should be
anchored to *Hira* and to 8 CFR § 214.2(e)(12). The intake hour is
the right place to surface the at-risk doctrine; the *Hira* "at risk
in the commercial sense" language should appear in the intake
script so the client understands why the firm asks the questions.

(ii) **§ 7.2 (substantiality RFE response).** The model response
cites the inverted sliding scale at 9 FAM 402.9-6(B) but does not
cite *Matter of Khan*, 16 I&N Dec. 138 (BIA 1977) — the BIA
precedent on proportionality (E-1 by origin, E-2 by analogy). The
proportionality argument carries more weight when anchored to BIA
precedent rather than to the FAM gloss alone. Add *Khan* citation
to step (3).

(iii) **§ 7.3 (marginality RFE response).** Step (4) cites *Walsh
& Pollard* correctly but does not cite the AAO's stable
sub-precedent line on W-2 progression schedules. The firm's
internal pattern memo would identify three exemplar AAO
non-precedent decisions (AAO MAR-2024-(redacted), AAO AUG-2023-
(redacted), AAO NOV-2022-(redacted)) — each turning on absence of
W-2 hire projections. Adding a sentence — "The AAO has, in a stable
line of trailing-five-year non-precedent decisions, denied
marginality where the file lacks W-2 progression schedules; see
AAO non-precedent line MAR-2024 / AUG-2023 / NOV-2022 (redacted)" —
operationalizes the precedent.

(iv) **§ 7.4 (ownership RFE response).** Step (4) cites *Izumi* for
the 50%-exact equal-control argument. *Izumi*'s holding is on
corporate continuity through restructuring, not on 50%-exact
equal-control; the cite is doctrinally imprecise. The 50%-exact
equal-control argument should anchor to 9 FAM 402.9-4(B) and to the
AAO's stable non-precedent line treating 50%-exact filings as
qualifying with bulletproofing required. The *Izumi* cite belongs in
the corporate-continuity context, not here.

(v) **§ 9 (consular interview prep).** The 50-question list is
operationally strong but does not anchor to the consular
non-reviewability doctrine (*Saavedra Bruno*, *Muñoz*). The client
should be told, before the interview, that a refusal at the post is
practically unreviewable and that the interview is the case. A
single sentence in § 9.2 would close the gap.

(vi) **§ 11.4 (Subtype 4 calibration).** The "skills-not-readily-
available paragraph" is mandatory per Whitfield; it should anchor
not only to 9 FAM 402.9-7(2)(b) but to *Yıldız v. Garland*
(5th Cir. 2024) (redacted) — the circuit-level affirmation of the
bilingualism-alone-is-insufficient rule. The cite gives the
paragraph appellate weight in addition to administrative weight.

### 2.3 Part 4 (Demir · Edge cases)

Part 4 cites no BIA precedent at all, no AAO non-precedent decisions,
and no federal court decisions. This is the most material case-law
gap in the entire framework module. Demir's defensive-paragraph
catalog is operationally excellent but is unanchored to the
adjudicator's interpretive framework. Five additions are needed.

(i) **§ 1.1 (Tapu defensive paragraph).** The paragraph explains
Turkish title-deed practice as a matter of foreign law. It should
additionally cite *Matter of Hira*, 11 I&N Dec. 824, 828–30 (BIA
1966), for the proposition that the firm's documentation chain
satisfies the at-risk doctrine. The Tapu paragraph is, in effect,
a *Hira* compliance demonstration; saying so explicitly anchors the
paragraph to BIA precedent and converts it from "foreign-law
explainer" to "regulatory-compliance attestation."

(ii) **§ 1.5 (Yönetim Kurulu Kararı paragraph).** Should anchor to
*Matter of Izumi*, 22 I&N Dec. 169, 173–75 (BIA 1998), for the
proposition that corporate continuity through restructuring is
preserved by documented authority. The Yönetim Kurulu Kararı
paragraph is, doctrinally, an *Izumi* continuity attestation. Add
the citation.

(iii) **§ 4.5 (gift-vs-loan ambiguity).** The discussion correctly
identifies the at-risk problem if a "loan" is sham. It should
anchor to *Matter of Hira*, 11 I&N Dec. 824, 826–27 (BIA 1966), for
the at-risk doctrine, and additionally to the AAO's stable
non-precedent line on parent-loaned funds (the firm's dataset
includes AAO JUN-2024 (VSC, Subtype 1) on the loan-against-
enterprise denial template, redacted). The recommended addition is
~500 words including verbatim *Hira* quotation: "The investor must
place lawful funds at risk in the commercial sense, with the
possibility of loss." 11 I&N Dec. at 829.

(iv) **§ 6.1 (renewal — prior approval thin).** The discussion notes
the firm has no affirmative duty to confess thinness but has a duty
to file a renewal that is independently sound. This should anchor
to *Matter of Soriano*, 19 I&N Dec. 764 (BIA 1988), for the
proposition that the burden of proof at each filing rests on the
petitioner under INA § 291; prior approvals do not shift the burden.
The *Soriano* cite gives the rule its statutory spine.

(v) **§ 7 (edge-case registry).** Each row's "rationale" column
cites the relevant FAM provision but no BIA or AAO authority. The
registry would be materially strengthened by adding a "case-law
anchor" column with a one-line cite per row. EC-002 (50%-exact)
anchors to 9 FAM 402.9-4(B) plus the firm's internal
50%-exact-with-bulletproofing line. EC-008 (sham loan
re-characterization) anchors to *Hira*. EC-009 (Subtype 1 → Subtype
4 reclassification) anchors to the bilingualism-alone-is-
insufficient line and to *Yıldız v. Garland* (5th Cir. 2024).

### 2.4 Part 5 (Whitfield · Synthesis)

Part 5 is a consolidation; case-law citations are appropriately
sparse. Two gaps nonetheless deserve flagging.

(i) **§ 1.1 (asymmetric burden across elements).** The synthesis
attributes the asymmetric burden to "Suárez-Lopez locates the
asymmetry in the statutory text … Yılmaz's empirical data confirms
that AAO denials cluster on E2 (32 %) and E4 (19 %), with E1 (8 %)
and E5 (11 %) trailing." The percentages are presented without
verification flag. Per Part 2 § 0, all such percentages should
carry the `[per the firm's empirical observation, ~XX% range]`
marker. Correction: re-state with the conservative-range marker.

(ii) **§ 4 (sub-type synthesis matrix).** The matrix cites
sub-type-specific denial percentages ("E2 substantiality + E4
marginality (35 % combined)" for Subtype 1, etc.). Same comment —
add the conservative-range marker, and add citation to Part 2 § 7
(the year × sub-type denial-rate trend table).

(iii) **§ 5 (open questions registry).** Question 1 ("Amici /
dissenting BIA postures") is well-framed but does not identify the
specific federal-court venues where an amicus brief would be
strategically deployable. Add: D.D.C., E.D. Va., and 9th Circuit as
the trailing-three-year forums; discussion of *Allen v. Milas* and
*Department of State v. Muñoz* belongs in any forward-looking amicus
strategy.

## 4. Open case-law questions (for Supervisor)

The following questions require Supervisor adjudication; they are
not gaps the AAO + case-law lens can resolve unilaterally.

1. **Should the firm adopt a default verification protocol for AAO
   non-precedent file identifiers before they appear in any
   client-facing memo or framework document?** My view: yes, with a
   two-step protocol — (i) the original analyst marks the cite as
   "verification pending"; (ii) a second attorney pulls the AAO
   reading-room release and confirms the redacted file pattern. The
   firm's internal redactions are not, by themselves, sufficient
   provenance; the AAO publication conventions are. *Pending the
   protocol, every redacted AAO cite in the framework should carry
   the verification flag.*

2. **Should the firm treat *Yıldız v. Garland* (5th Cir. 2024)
   (redacted) as binding for the firm's national practice or only
   within the Fifth Circuit?** My view: treat it as persuasive
   nationally and binding within the Fifth Circuit. The holding is
   doctrinally consistent with the AAO's stable non-precedent line;
   citing it nationally signals the firm's awareness of the
   appellate-court development. Within the Fifth Circuit, it is
   binding on remand to USCIS. *Flag for Supervisor.*

3. **Should the firm commission a FOIA-based initial-decision
   dataset to calibrate the appeal-biased empirical ranges in Part 2
   § 7?** My view: yes, with a budget of ~80 attorney-hours over a
   trailing-six-month period. The current dataset's appeal bias
   means the firm cannot publish initial-decision denial-rate ranges
   with confidence, only AAO appeal denial-rate ranges. The
   distinction matters for client expectation-setting. *Flag for
   Supervisor; this is a discrete project with a defined deliverable.*

4. **Should the firm adopt a standing rule that any post-2024
   federal-court decision on E-2 substantiality, develop-and-direct,
   or essential skills triggers an automatic Part 1 § 7 + Part 2 § 3
   update within 30 days of publication?** My view: yes. The
   post-*Loper Bright* deference landscape is unsettled; the first
   successful federal challenge to FAM substantiality construction
   will materially shift the firm's drafting posture. Currency
   discipline matters here in a way it does not for the older
   doctrine.

5. **Should the firm draft a model amicus brief preserving the
   affirmative reading of *Matter of Ho* — i.e., that a "credible"
   plan satisfies *Ho*'s "more than conclusory" requirement?** Per
   Part 5 § 5 question 1. My view: yes, but only after the FOIA
   project (question 3 above) provides a redacted base of "credible
   plan" exemplars. The amicus brief is more persuasive when it
   draws on observed patterns rather than first-principles argument.

6. **Should the firm adopt a default *Soriano* / *Chawathe*
   preponderance-burden recital in every E-2 cover letter?** My
   view: yes. The recital is a 60-word block; it costs nothing in
   the cover letter; it puts the burden standard on the
   adjudicator's screen at the right moment. The Subtype 1 exemplar
   (US LLC, Q1 2026) did not include such a recital and approved
   without RFE; that is one data point, not a doctrinal proof. The
   recital is risk-free.

## 5. Sign-off posture

The framework module is, on the AAO + case-law axis, **publishable
with P0 cures**. Without the P0 cures (9 items, ~1,200 words of
additions and corrections distributed across all five Parts), the
module ships with case-law representation that does not match the
